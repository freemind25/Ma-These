    15→   564→THE SCOPE OF SCIENTIFIC WRITING
    16→   565→The term scientific writing commonly denotes the reporting of original research
    17→   566→in journals through scientific papers that follow a standard format. In its broader
    18→   567→sense, scientific writing also includes communication about science through
    19→   568→other types of journal articles, such as review papers summarizing and integrat-
    20→   569→ing previously published research. And in a still broader sense, it includes other
    21→   570→types of professional communication by scientists—for example, grant propos-
    22→   571→als, oral presentations, and poster presentations. Related endeavors include
    23→   572→writing about science for the public, sometimes called science writing.
    24→   573→THE NEED FOR CLARITY
    25→   574→The key characteristic of scientific writing is clarity. Successful scientific
    26→   575→experimentation is the result of a clear mind attacking a clearly stated problem
    27→   576→and producing clearly stated conclusions. Ideally, clarity should be a character-
    28→   577→```
    29→   578→istic of any type of communication; however, when something is being said for
    30→   579→```
    31→   580→the first time, clarity is essential. Most scientific papers, those published in our
    32→   581→primary research journals, are accepted for publication precisely because they
    33→   582→contribute new knowledge. Hence, we should demand absolute clarity in sci-
    34→   583→entific writing.

    38→   587→RECEIVING THE SIGNALS
    39→   588→Many people have no doubt heard this question: If a tree falls in the forest and
    40→   589→there is no one there to hear it fall, does it make a sound? The correct answer
    41→   590→is no. Sound is more than pressure waves, and indeed there can be no sound
    42→   591→without someone to hear it.
    43→   592→Similarly, scientific communication is a two-way process. Just as a signal of
    44→   593→```
    45→   594→any kind is useless unless it is perceived, a published scientific paper (signal)
    46→   595→```
    47→   596→is useless unless it is both received and understood by its intended audience.
    48→   597→Thus we can restate the axiom of science as follows: A scientific experiment is
    49→   598→not complete until the results have been published and understood. A pub-
    50→   599→lished paper is no more than pressure waves unless it is understood. Too
    51→   600→many scientific papers fall silently in the woods.
    52→   601→UNDERSTANDING THE SIGNALS
    53→   602→Scientific writing is the transmission of a clear signal to a recipient. The words
    54→   603→of the signal should be as clear, simple, and well ordered as possible. In
    55→   604→scientific writing, there is little need for ornamentation. Flowery literary
    56→   605→embellishments—metaphors, similes, idiomatic expressions—are very likely
    57→   606→to cause confusion and should seldom be used in research papers.
    58→   607→Science is simply too important to be communicated in anything other
    59→   608→than words that have a certain meaning. And the meaning should be clear and
    60→   609→certain not just to peers of the authors, but also to students just embarking on
    61→   610→their careers, to scientists reading outside their own narrow disciplines, and
    62→   611→```
    63→   612→especially to those readers (most readers today) whose native language is other
    64→   613→```
    65→   614→than English.
    66→   615→Many kinds of writing are designed for entertainment. Scientific writing
    67→   616→has a different purpose: to communicate new scientific findings. Scientific
    68→   617→writing should be as clear and simple as possible.
    69→   618→UNDERSTANDING THE CONTEXT
    70→   619→What is clear to a recipient depends both on what is transmitted and how the
    71→   620→recipient interprets it. Therefore, communicating clearly requires awareness
    72→   621→of what the recipient brings. What is the recipient’s background? What is the
    73→   622→recipient seeking? How does the recipient expect the writing to be organized?
    74→   623→Clarity in scientific writing requires attentiveness to such questions. As
    75→   624→communication professionals advise, know your audience. Also know the

   284→   833→THE IMRAD STORY
   285→   834→The early journals published papers that we call descriptive. Typically, a scien-
   286→   835→tist would report, “First, I saw this, and then I saw that,” or “First, I did this,
   287→   836→and then I did that.” Often the observations were in simple chronological
   288→   837→order.
   289→   838→This descriptive style was appropriate for the kind of science then being
   290→   839→reported. In fact, this straightforward style of reporting still is sometimes used
   291→   840→in “letters” journals, case reports in medicine, geological surveys, and other
   292→   841→publications.
   293→   842→By the second half of the nineteenth century, science was beginning to move
   294→   843→fast and in increasingly sophisticated ways. Microbiology serves as an example.
   295→   844→Especially through the work of Louis Pasteur, who confirmed the germ theory
   296→   845→of disease and developed pure-culture methods of studying microorganisms,
   297→   846→both science and the reporting of science made great advances.
   298→   847→At this time, methodology became all-important. To quiet his critics, many
   299→   848→of whom were fanatic believers in the theory of spontaneous generation, Pas-
   300→   849→teur found it necessary to describe his experiments in exquisite detail. Because
   301→   850→reasonably competent peers could reproduce Pasteur’s experiments, the prin-
   302→   851→ciple of reproducibility of experiments became a fundamental tenet of the phi-
   303→   852→losophy of science, and a separate methods section led the way toward the
   304→   853→highly structured IMRAD format.
