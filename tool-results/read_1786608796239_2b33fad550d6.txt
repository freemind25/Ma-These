   303→A Note to Students
   304→Now in its seventh edition, A Manual for Writers of Research Papers, Theses, and
   305→Dissertations has helped generations of students successfully research, write, and submit their
   306→papers. Most commonly known to its dedicated users as “Turabian,” the name of the original
   307→author, A Manual for Writers is the authoritative student resource on “Chicago style.”
   308→If you are writing a research paper, you may be told to follow Chicago style for citations
   309→and for issues of mechanics, such as capitalization and abbreviations. Chicago style is widely
   310→used by students in all disciplines. For citations, you may use one of two styles recommended
   311→by Chicago. In the humanities and some social sciences, you will likely use notesbibliography style, while in the natural and physical sciences (and some social sciences) you
   312→may use parenthetical citations–reference list (or “author-date”) style. A Manual for Writers
   313→explains and illustrates both styles.
   314→In addition to detailed information on Chicago style, this seventh edition includes a new
   315→part by Wayne C. Booth, Gregory G. Colomb, and Joseph M. Williams that covers every step
   316→of the research and writing process. This section provides practical advice to help you
   317→formulate the right questions, read critically, build arguments, and revise your draft.

   319→Preface
   320→Students writing research papers, theses, and dissertations in today's colleges and universities
   321→inhabit a world filled with electronic technologies that were unimagined in 1937—the year
   322→Kate L. Turabian, University of Chicago's dissertation secretary, assembled a booklet of
   323→guidelines for student writers. The availability of Internet sources and word-processing
   324→software has changed the way students conduct research and write up the results. But these
   325→technologies have not altered the basic task of the student writer: doing well-designed
   326→research and presenting it clearly and accurately, while following accepted academic
   327→standards for citation, style, and format.
   328→Turabian's 1937 booklet reflected guidelines found in A Manual of Style, 10th edition—an
   329→already classic resource for writers and editors published by the University of Chicago Press.
   330→The Press began distributing Turabian's booklet in 1947 and first published the work in book
   331→form in 1955, under the title A Manual for Writers of Term Papers, Theses, and Dissertations.
   332→Turabian revised the work twice more, updating it to meet students' needs and to reflect the
   333→
   334→www.itpub.net
   335→
   336→latest recommendations of the Manual of Style. In time, Turabian's book has become a
   337→standard reference for students of all levels at universities and colleges across the country.
   338→Turabian died in 1987 at age ninety-four, a few months after publication of the fifth edition.
   339→For that edition, as well as the sixth (1996), the Press invited editorial staff to carry out the
   340→revisions.
   341→For this seventh edition, Wayne C. Booth, Gregory G. Colomb, and Joseph M. Williams
   342→have expanded the focus of the book. The new part 1, “Research and Writing: From Planning
   343→to Production,” is adapted from their Craft of Research (Chicago: University of Chicago
   344→Press, 2003). This part offers a step-by-step guide to the process of research and its reporting,
   345→a topic not previously covered in this manual but inseparable from source citation, writing
   346→style, and the mechanics of paper preparation. Among the topics covered are the nature of
   347→research, finding and engaging sources, taking notes, developing an argument, drafting and
   348→revising, and presenting evidence in tables and figures. Also included is a discussion of
   349→presenting research in alternative forums. In this part, the authors write in a familiar, collegial
   350→voice to engage readers in a complex topic. Students undertaking research projects at all
   351→levels will benefit from reading this part, though advanced researchers may wish to skim
   352→chapters 1–4.
   353→The rest of the book covers the same topics as past editions, but has been extensively
   354→revised to follow the recommendations in The Chicago Manual of Style, 15th edition (2003),
   355→to incorporate current technology as it affects all aspects of student writing, to provide
   356→updated examples, and to be easier to read and use.
   357→Reflecting the close connection between the research process and the need for careful and
   358→appropriate citation form, “Source Citation” now appears as part 2 of the manual. In this part,
   359→chapter 15 offers an overview of scholarly citation, including its relationship not just to good
   360→research practices but to the ethics of research. Students using notes-bibliography style for
   361→citations (common in the humanities and some social sciences) should then read chapter 16
   362→for a discussion of the basic form for citations and consult chapter 17 as needed for a wide
   363→range of source examples. Students using parenthetical citations–reference list style (common
   364→in most social sciences and in the natural and physical sciences) will find the same types of
   365→information in chapters 18 and 19. Both sets of chapters include updated examples and new
   366→coverage of how to cite online and other electronic sources.
   367→Part 3, “Style,” addresses issues that occupied the first half of previous editions of the
   368→manual. Coverage of spelling and punctuation has been divided into separate chapters, as has
   369→treatment of numbers and abbreviations. The chapter on names, special terms, and titles of
   370→works has been expanded. The final two chapters in this section treat the mechanics of using
   371→quotations and graphics (tables and figures), topics that are discussed from a rhetorical
   372→perspective in part 1. Student writers may wish to read these chapters in their entirety or
   373→consult them for guidance on particular points.
   374→The recommendations in parts 2 and 3 diverge in a few instances from those in The
   375→Chicago Manual of Style, but the differences are matters of degree, not substance. In certain
   376→cases, this manual recommends just one editorial style where CMOS recommends two or
   377→more. Sometimes the choice is a matter of simplicity (as in the rules for headline-style
   378→capitalization presented in chapter 22); other times it reflects what is appropriate for student
   379→papers, as opposed to published works (as in the requirement of access dates with all citations
   380→from online sources). The chapters on citation include new types of sources, such as Weblogs,
   381→
   382→that have emerged since 2003 and thus are not treated in the current edition of CMOS.
   383→These recommendations logically extend principles set forth in CMOS.
   384→The appendix gathers in one place the material on paper format and submission that formed
   385→the core of Kate Turabian's original booklet. In the years since, this material has become the
   386→primary authority for dissertation offices throughout the nation. In revising this material, the
   387→Press sought the advice of dissertation officials at a variety of public and private universities,
   388→including those named in the acknowledgments section. While continuing to emphasize the
   389→importance of consistency, the guidelines now allow more flexibility in matters such as the
   390→placement of page numbers and the typography of titles, reflecting the capabilities of current
   391→word-processing software. The sample pages presented are new and are adapted from
   392→exemplary dissertations submitted to the University of Chicago since 2000. This appendix is
   393→intended primarily for students writing PhD dissertations and master's and undergraduate
   394→theses, but the sections on format requirements and electronic file preparation also apply to
   395→those writing class papers.
   396→The guidelines in this manual offer practical solutions to a wide range of issues
   397→encountered by student writers, but they may be supplemented—or even overruled—by the
   398→conventions of specific disciplines or the preferences of particular institutions or departments.
   399→All of the chapters on style and format remind students to review the requirements of their
   400→university, department, or instructor, which take precedence over the guidelines presented
   401→here. The expanded bibliography, organized by subject area, lists sources for research and
   402→style issues specific to particular disciplines.

   462→Overview of Part I
   463→We know how challenged you can feel when you start a substantial research project, whether
   464→it's a PhD dissertation, a BA or master's thesis, or just a long class paper. But you can handle
   465→any project if you break it into its parts, then work on them one step at a time. This part shows
   466→you how to do that.
   467→
   468→We first discuss the aims of research and what readers will expect of any research report.
   469→Then we focus on how to find a research question whose answer is worth your time and your
   470→readers' attention; how to find and use information from sources to back up your answer; then
   471→how to plan, draft, and revise your report so your readers will think your answer is based on
   472→sound reasoning and reliable evidence.
   473→Several themes run through this part.
   474→You can't plunge into a project blindly; you must plan it, then keep the whole process in
   475→mind as you take each step. So think big, but break the process down into small goals that
   476→you can meet one at a time.
   477→Your best research will begin with a question that you want to answer. But you must then
   478→imagine readers asking a question of their own: So what if you don't answer it? Why should
   479→I care?
   480→From the outset, you should try to write every day, not just to take notes on your sources
   481→but to clarify what you think of them. You should also write down your own developing
   482→ideas to get them out of the cozy warmth of your head into the cold light of day, where you
   483→can see if they still make sense. You probably won't use much of this writing in your final
   484→draft, but it is essential preparation for it.
   485→No matter how carefully you do your research, readers will judge it by how well you
   486→report it, so you must know what they will look for in a clearly written report that earns
   487→their respect.
   488→If you're an advanced researcher, skim chapters 1–4. You will see there much that's
   489→familiar, but if you're also teaching, it may help you explain what you know to your students
   490→more effectively. (Many experienced researchers report that chapters 5–12 have helped them
   491→not only explain to others how to do research and report it, but also to draft and revise their
   492→own reports more quickly and effectively.)
   493→If you're just starting your career in research, you'll find every chapter of part 1 useful.
   494→Skim it all for an overview of the process; then as you work through your project, reread
   495→chapters relevant to your immediate task.
   496→You may feel that the steps described here are too many to remember, but you can manage
   497→them if you take them one at a time, and as you do more research, they'll become habits of
   498→mind. Don't think, however, that you must follow these steps in exactly the order we present
   499→them. Researchers regularly think ahead to future steps as they work through earlier ones and
   500→revisit earlier steps as they deal with a later one. (That explains why we so often refer you
   501→ahead to anticipate a later stage in the process and back to revisit an earlier one.) And even the
   502→most systematic researcher has unexpected insights that send her off in a new direction. Work
   503→from a plan, but be ready to depart from it, even to discard it for a new one.
   504→If you're a very new researcher, you may also think that some matters we discuss are
   505→beyond your immediate needs. We know that a ten-page class paper differs from a PhD
   506→dissertation. But both require a kind of thinking that even the newest researcher can start
   507→practicing. You begin your journey toward full competence when you not only know what lies
   508→
   509→www.itpub.net
   510→
   511→ahead but can also start practicing the skills that experienced researchers began to learn
   512→when they were where you are now.
   513→No book can prepare you for every aspect of a research project. And this one won't help
   514→you with the specific methodologies in fields such as psychology, economics, or philosophy,
   515→much less physics, chemistry, or biology. Nor does it tell you how to adapt what you learn
   516→about academic research to business or professional settings.
   517→But it does provide an overview of the processes and habits of mind that underlie all
   518→research, wherever it's done, and of the plans you must make to assemble a report, draft it, and
   519→revise it. With that knowledge and help from your teachers, you'll come to feel in control of
   520→your projects, not intimidated by them, and eventually you'll learn to manage even the most
   521→complex projects on your own, in both the academic and the professional worlds.
   522→The first step in learning the skills of sound research is to understand how experienced
   523→researchers think about its aims.

   552→The Special Challenge of Conceptual Questions: Answering So What?
   553→
   554→You do research every time you ask a question and look for facts to answer it, whether the
   555→question is as simple as finding a plumber or as profound as discovering the origin of life.
   556→When only you care about the answer or when others need just a quick report of it, you
   557→probably won't write it out. But you must report your research in writing when others will
   558→accept your claims only after they study how you reached them. In fact, reports of research
   559→tell us most of what we can reliably believe about our world—that once there were dinosaurs,
   560→that germs cause disease, even that the earth is round.
   561→
   562→You may think your report will add little to the world's knowledge. Maybe so. But done
   563→well, it will add a lot to yours and to your ability to do the next report. You may also think
   564→that your future lies not in scholarly research but in business or a profession. But research is
   565→as important outside the academy as in, and in most ways it is the same. So as you practice the
   566→craft of academic research now, you prepare yourself to do research that one day will be
   567→important at least to those you work with, perhaps to us all.
   568→As you learn to do your own research, you also learn to use—and judge—that of others. In
   569→every profession, researchers must read and evaluate reports before they make a decision, a
   570→job you'll do better only after you've learned how others will judge yours. This book focuses
   571→on research in the academic world, but every day, we read or hear about research that can
   572→affect our lives. Before we believe those reports, though, we must think about them critically
   573→to determine whether they are based on evidence and reasoning that we can trust.
   574→To be sure, we can reach good conclusions in ways other than through reasons and
   575→evidence: we can rely on tradition and authority or on intuition, spiritual insight, even on our
   576→most visceral emotions. But when we try to explain to others not just why we believe our
   577→claims but why they should too, we must do more than just state an opinion and describe our
   578→feelings.
   579→That is how a research report differs from other kinds of persuasive writing: it must rest on
   580→shared facts that readers accept as truths independent of your feelings and beliefs. They must
   581→be able to follow your reasoning from evidence that they accept to the claim you draw from it.
   582→Your success as a researcher thus depends not just on how well you gather and analyze data,
   583→but on how clearly you report your reasoning so that your readers can test and judge it before
   584→making your claims part of their knowledge and understanding.

   586→1.1 How Researchers Think about Their Aims
   587→All researchers gather facts and information, what we're calling data. But depending on their
   588→aims and experience, they use those data in different ways. Some researchers gather data on a
   589→topic—stories about the Battle of the Alamo, for example—just to satisfy a personal interest
   590→(or a teacher's assignment).
   591→Most researchers, however, want us to know more than just facts. So they don't look for
   592→just any data on a topic; they look for specific data that they can use as evidence to test and
   593→support an answer to a question that their topic inspired them to ask, such as Why has the
   594→Alamo story become a national legend?
   595→Experienced researchers, however, know that they must do more than convince us that their
   596→answer is sound. They must also show us why their question was worth asking, how its
   597→answer helps us understand some bigger issue in a new way. If we can figure out why the
   598→Alamo story has become a national legend, we might then answer a larger question: How
   599→have regional myths shaped our national character?
   600→You can judge how closely your thinking tracks that of an experienced researcher by
   601→describing your project in a sentence like this:
   602→
   603→www.itpub.net
   604→
   605→1. I am working on the topic of X (stories about the Battle of the Alamo),
   606→2. because I want to find out Y (why its story became a national legend),
   607→3. so that I can help others understand Z (how such regional myths have shaped our national
   608→character).
   609→
   610→That sentence is worth a close look because it describes not just the progress of your research
   611→but your personal growth as a researcher.
   612→1. “I am working on the topic of . . . ” Researchers often begin with a simple topic like the
   613→Battle of the Alamo, either because it was assigned, because something about it puzzles
   614→them, or because it merely sparks an interest. But inexperienced researchers too often stop
   615→there, leaving themselves with nothing but a topic to guide their work. They mound up
   616→hundreds of notes, but have no way to decide what data to keep and what to discard. When
   617→it comes time to write, they dump everything into a report that reads like a grab bag of
   618→random facts. If those facts are new to readers who happen to be interested in the topic,
   619→they might read the report. But even those readers will want to know what those facts add
   620→up to.
   621→2. “ . . . because I want to find out how or why . . . ” More experienced researchers usually
   622→begin not with just a topic but with a research question, such as Why has the story of the
   623→Alamo become a national legend? And they know that readers will think their facts add up
   624→to something only when those facts serve as evidence to support its answer. Indeed, only
   625→with a question can a researcher know which facts to look for and which to keep—not just
   626→those that support an answer, but also those that test or even discredit it. When he thinks he
   627→has enough evidence to support his answer and can respond to data that seem to contradict
   628→it, he writes a report first to test his own thinking, then to share his answer with others so
   629→that they can test it too.
   630→3. “ . . . so that I can help others understand . . . ” The most successful researchers,
   631→however, realize that readers want to know not only that an answer is sound but why the
   632→question was worth asking. So they anticipate that readers will ask a question of their own:
   633→So what? Why should I care why the Alamo story has become a legend? That So what? can
   634→vex even the most experienced researcher, but every researcher must try to answer it
   635→before it's asked: If we can find that out, we might better understand the bigger question of
   636→how such stories shape our national character.
   637→But a shrewd researcher doesn't stop there. She anticipates her readers' asking So what?
   638→again by looking for another, still larger answer: And if we can understand what has shaped
   639→our national character, we might understand better who we Americans think we are. And
   640→before you ask, when we know that, we might better understand why others in the world judge
   641→us as they do. The most successful researchers know that readers care about a question only
   642→when they think that its answer might encourage them to say not So what? but That's worth
   643→knowing!
   644→In short, not all questions are equally good. We might ask how many cats slept in the
   645→Alamo the night before the battle, but so what if we find out? It is hard to see how an answer
   646→would help us think about any larger issue worth understanding, so it's a question that's
   647→probably not worth asking (though as we'll see, we could be wrong about that).
   648→
   649→1.2 Three Kinds of Questions That Researchers Ask
   650→Experienced researchers also know that different readers expect them to ask and answer
   651→different kinds of questions. The most common questions in academic work are conceptual.
   652→The ones most common in the professions are practical.
   653→1.2.1 Conceptual Questions: What Should We Think?
   654→A question is conceptual when your answer to So what? doesn't tell readers what to do, but
   655→helps them understand some issue:
   656→1. I am working on the topic of X,
   657→2. because I want to find out how/why/whether Y, (So what if you do?)
   658→3. so that I can help others understand how/why/whether Z.
   659→
   660→If you were explaining your research, the conversation might go like this:
   661→I'm working on the topic of risk evaluation.
   662→Why?
   663→Because I want to find out how ordinary people evaluate the risk that they will be hurt by terrorism.
   664→So what if you do?
   665→Once I do, we might better understand the bigger question of how emotional and rational factors interact to
   666→influence the way ordinary thinkers think about risk.
   667→
   668→Researchers in the humanities and the social and natural sciences work mostly on
   669→conceptual questions, such as How did Shakespeare's political environment influence his
   670→plays? What caused the extinction of most large North American mammals? What are comets
   671→made of? The answers to those questions don't tell us how to change the world, but they do
   672→help us understand it better.
   673→To be sure, the answer to a conceptual question often turns out to be unexpectedly relevant
   674→to solving a practical problem. And before we can solve any important practical problem, we
   675→usually must do conceptual research to understand it better. But in most of the academic
   676→world, the primary aim of most researchers is only to improve our understanding.
   677→1.2.2 Practical Questions: What Should We Do?
   678→You pose a different kind of question—call it a practical one—when your answer to So what?
   679→tells readers what to do to change or fix some troublesome or at least improvable situation:
   680→1. I am working on the topic of X,
   681→2. because I want to find out Y, (So what if you do?)
   682→3. so that I can tell readers what to do to fix/improve Z.
   683→
   684→You would explain your work on a practical question like this:
   685→
   686→www.itpub.net
   687→
   688→I'm working on the topic of communicating risk effectively.
   689→Why?
   690→Because I want to find out what psychological factors cause ordinary Americans to exaggerate their personal risk
   691→from a terrorist attack.
   692→So what if you do?
   693→Then I can tell the government how to counteract those factors when they communicate with the public about the
   694→real risk of terrorism.
   695→
   696→Practical questions are most common outside the academic world, especially in business. In
   697→academic fields such as health care and engineering, researchers sometimes ask practical
   698→questions, but more often they ask a third kind of question that's neither purely practical nor
   699→purely conceptual: call it an applied research question.
   700→1.2.3 Applied Questions: What Must We Understand before We Know What to Do?
   701→Often, we know we must do something to solve a practical problem, but before we can know
   702→what that is, we must do research to understand the problem better. We can call that kind of
   703→research applied. With this middle kind of question, the third step raises a question whose
   704→answer is not the solution to a practical problem, but only a step toward it.
   705→I want to find out how Americans have changed their daily lives in response to the terrorist attacks on 9/11.
   706→So what if you do?
   707→Then we can understand the psychological factors that cause ordinary Americans to exaggerate their personal
   708→risk from a terrorist attack.
   709→So what if you do?
   710→Then we can understand how to reduce the effects of those psychological factors.
   711→So what if you do?
   712→Then perhaps the government can use that information to communicate more effectively the real risk from
   713→terrorism.
   714→
   715→Applied questions are common in academic fields such as business, engineering, and
   716→medicine and in companies and government agencies that do research to understand what
   717→must be known before they can solve a problem.
   718→1.2.4 Choosing the Right Kind of Question
   719→Some new researchers dislike purely conceptual research questions because they think they're
   720→too “theoretical” or irrelevant to the “real” world. So they try to cobble an implausible
   721→practical use onto a conceptual answer: When we know how race shaped the political impact
   722→of the Alamo stories, we can understand how racism has been used to foster patriotism and
   723→thereby eliminate racist appeals to patriotism in relation to conflicts in the Middle East.
   724→
   725→That impulse is understandable. But unless you've been assigned an applied or practical
   726→problem, resist it. You are unlikely to solve any significant practical problem in a class paper,
   727→and in any case, most of the academic world sees its mission not as fixing the problems of the
   728→world directly, but as understanding them better (which may or may not help fix them).
   729→1.2.5 The Special Challenge of Conceptual Questions: Answering So What?
   730→With most practical questions, we don't have to answer So what? because the benefit is
   731→usually obvious. Even most applied questions imply the practical benefits of their answers:
   732→few readers would question why a researcher is trying to understand what causes Alzheimer's.
   733→With conceptual questions, however, the answer to So what? is often not obvious at all, even
   734→to an experienced researcher: So what if Shakespeare had Lady Macbeth die offstage rather
   735→than on? So what if some cultures use masks in their religious rituals and others don't? Why
   736→is it important to know that?
   737→For a research paper in an introductory course, your instructor may be satisfied with any
   738→plausible answer to So what? So if early in your research career you find yourself struggling
   739→with that question, don't take it as a sign of failure, much less as evidence that you're not ready
   740→to do the work. In fact, you might not discover the answer to So what? until you've drafted
   741→your report, maybe not even until you've finished it. And even then, maybe the answer will
   742→matter only to you.
   743→But if your project is a thesis or dissertation, it's not just an advisor that you have to satisfy.
   744→Your answer must also satisfy those in your field (represented by your advisor) who will
   745→judge your work not just by the quality of your answer, but by the significance of your
   746→question. Experienced researchers know that some readers, perhaps many, will read their
   747→report and think I don't agree. They accept that as an inevitable part of sharing research on
   748→significant issues. What they can't accept is I don't care.
   749→So as hard as it will be, the more often you imagine others asking So what? and the more
   750→often you try to answer it, if only to your own satisfaction, the more confident you can be that
   751→eventually you'll learn to succeed at every experienced researcher's toughest task—to
   752→convince your readers that your report is worth their time. (In chapter 10, we discuss how to
   753→write an introduction that motivates your readers at least to start reading your report.)

   816→A research project is more than collecting data. You start it before you log on to the Internet
   817→or head for the library, and you continue it long after you have all the data you think you need.
   818→In that process, you face countless specific tasks, but they all aim at just five general goals.
   819→You must do the following:
   820→Ask a question worth answering.
   821→Find an answer that you can support with good reasons.
   822→Find reliable evidence to support your reasons.
   823→Draft a report that makes a good case for your answer.
   824→Revise that draft until readers will think you met the first four goals.
   825→
   826→You might even post those five goals in your workspace.
   827→Research projects would be easy if you could march straight through those steps. But as
   828→you've discovered (or soon will), research and its reporting are never straightforward. As you
   829→do one task, you'll have to look ahead to others or revisit an earlier one. You'll change topics
   830→as you read, search for more data as you draft, perhaps even discover a new question as you
   831→revise. Research is looping, messy, and unpredictable. But it's manageable if you have a plan,
   832→even when you know you'll depart from it.

   834→2.1 Find a Question in Your Topic
   835→Researchers begin projects in different ways. Many experienced ones begin with a question
   836→that others in their field want to answer: What caused the extinction of most large North
   837→American mammals? Others begin with just a vague intellectual itch that they have to scratch.
   838→They might not know what puzzles them about giant sloths and mastodons, but they're willing
   839→to spend time finding out whether they can translate their itch into a question worth
   840→answering.
   841→They know, moreover, that the best research question is not one whose answer others want
   842→to know just for its own sake; it is one that helps them understand some larger issue (So what?
   843→again). For example, if we knew why North American sloths disappeared, we might be able to
   844→answer a larger question that puzzles many historical anthropologists: Did early Native
   845→Americans live in harmony with nature, as some believe, or did they hunt its largest creatures
   846→to extinction? (And if we knew that, then we might also understand. . . .)
   847→Then there are those questions that just pop into a researcher's mind with no hint of where
   848→they'll lead, sometimes about matters so seemingly trivial that only the researcher thinks
   849→they're worth answering: Why does a coffee spill dry up in the form of a ring? Such a question
   850→might lead nowhere, but you can't know that until you see its answer. In fact, the scientist
   851→puzzled by coffee rings made discoveries about the behavior of fluids that others in his field
   852→thought important—and that paint manufacturers found valuable. So who knows where you
   853→might go with a question like How many cats slept in the Alamo the night before the battle?
   854→You can't know until you get there.
   855→In fact, a researcher's most valuable ability is the knack of being puzzled by ordinary
   856→things: like the shape of coffee rings; or why Shakespeare has Lady Macbeth die offstage
   857→rather than on; or why your eyebrows don't grow as long as the hair on your head. Cultivate
   858→the ability to see what's odd in the commonplace and you'll never lack for research projects, as
   859→either a student or a professional.
   860→If you have a topic, skip to 2.1.3 to find questions in it. If you already have a question or
   861→two, skip to 2.1.4 to test them by the criteria listed there. If you're still looking for a topic,
   862→here's a plan to help you search for one.

   893→If you pick a topic whose name sounds like an encyclopedia entry—bridges, birds, masks—
   894→you'll find so many sources that you could spend a lifetime reading them. You must carve out
   895→of your topic a manageable piece. You can start before you head to the library by limiting
   896→your topic to reflect a special interest in it: What is it about, say, masks that made you choose
   897→them? What particular aspect of them interests or puzzles you? Think about your topic in a
   898→context that you know something about, then add words and phrases to reflect that

   901→masks in religious ceremonies

   907→Begin with an overview of your topic in a general encyclopedia (in the bibliography, see
   908→items in category 2 in the general references); then read about it in a specialized one (see
   909→items in category 2 in your field).

   912→bibliography, see items in category 4 in your field). That will also give you a start on a

   917→how such debates are conducted (for more on this, see 3.1.2).
   918→2.1.3 Question Your Topic
   919→Do this not just once, early on, but throughout your project. Ask questions as you read,
   920→especially how and why (see also 4.1.1–4.1.2). Try the following kinds of questions (the
   921→categories are loose and overlap, so don't worry about keeping them distinct).
   922→1. Ask how the topic fits into a larger context (historical, social, cultural, geographic,
   923→functional, economic, and so on):
   924→How does your topic fit into a larger story? What came before masks? How did masks
   925→come into being? Why? What changes have they caused in other parts of their social or
   926→geographic setting? How and why did that happen? Why have masks become a part of
   927→Halloween? How and why have masks helped make Halloween the biggest American
   928→holiday after Christmas?
   929→How is your topic a functioning part of a larger system? How do masks reflect the
   930→values of specific societies and cultures? What roles do masks play in Hopi dances? In
   931→scary movies? In masquerade parties? For what purposes are masks used other than
   932→disguise? How has the booming market for kachina masks influenced traditional
   933→designs?
   934→How does your topic compare to and contrast with other topics like it? How do masks
   935→
   936→www.itpub.net
   937→
   938→in Native American ceremonies differ from those in Africa? What do Halloween masks
   939→have to do with Mardi Gras masks? How are masks and cosmetic surgery alike?
   940→2. Ask questions about the nature of the thing itself, as an independent entity:
   941→How has your topic changed through time? Why? What is its future? How have
   942→Halloween masks changed? Why? How have Native American masks changed? Why?
   943→How do the parts of your topic fit together as a system? What parts of a mask are
   944→most significant in Hopi ceremonies? Why? Why do some masks cover only the eyes?
   945→Why do so few masks cover just the bottom half of the face?
   946→How many different categories of your topic are there? What are the different kinds of
   947→Halloween masks? What are the different qualities of masks? What are the different
   948→functions of Halloween masks?
   949→3. Turn positive questions into a negative ones: Why have masks not become a part of
   950→Christmas? How do Native American masks not differ from those in Africa? What parts of
   951→masks are typically not significant in religious ceremonies?
   952→4. Ask speculative questions: Why are masks common in African religions but not in
   953→Western ones? Why are children more comfortable wearing Halloween masks than are
   954→most adults? Why don't hunters in camouflage wear masks?
   955→5. Ask What if? questions: How would things be different if your topic never existed,
   956→disappeared, or were put into a new context? What if no one ever wore masks except for
   957→safety reasons? What if everyone wore masks in public? What if movies and TV were like
   958→Greek plays and all the actors wore masks? What if it were customary to wear masks on
   959→blind dates? In marriage ceremonies? At funerals?
   960→6. Ask questions that reflect disagreements with a source: If a source makes a claim you
   961→think is weakly supported or even wrong, make that disagreement a question (see also
   962→4.1.2). Martinez claims that carnival masks uniquely allow wearers to escape social
   963→norms. But I think religious masks also allow wearers to escape from the material realm to
   964→the spiritual. Is there a larger pattern of all masks creating a sense of alternative forms of
   965→social or spiritual life?
   966→7. Ask questions that build on agreement: If a source offers a claim you think is persuasive,
   967→ask questions that extend its reach (see also 4.1.1). Elias shows that masked balls became
   968→popular in eighteenth-century London in response to anxiety about social mobility. Is the
   969→same anxiety responsible for similar developments in other European capitals? You can
   970→also ask a question that supports the same claim with additional evidence. Elias supports
   971→his claim about masked balls entirely with published sources. Is it also supported by
   972→evidence from unpublished sources such as letters and diaries?
   973→8. Ask questions analogous to those that others have asked about similar topics. Smith
   974→analyzed the Battle of Gettysburg from an economic point of view. What would an
   975→economic analysis of the Battle of the Alamo turn up?
   976→
   977→9. Look for questions that other researchers pose but don't answer. Many journal articles
   978→end with a paragraph or two about open questions, ideas for more research, and so on. You
   979→might not be able to do all the research they suggest, but you might carve out a piece of it.
   980→10. Find a Web discussion list on your topic, then “lurk,” just reading the exchanges to
   981→understand the kinds of questions those on the list discuss. If you can't find a list using a
   982→search engine, ask a teacher or visit the Web site of professional organizations in your
   983→field. Look for questions that spark your interest. You can even ask a question of the list so
   984→long as it is very specific and narrowly focused, but wait until you see whether questions
   985→from students are welcomed.
   986→2.1.4 Evaluate Your Questions
   987→Not all answers are equally useful, so evaluate your questions and scrap those that are
   988→unlikely to yield interesting answers. Reconsider when the following is true.
   989→1. You can answer the question too easily.
   990→You can look it up: What masks are used in Navajo dances?
   991→You can summarize a source: What does Fisher say about masks and fears?
   992→2. You can't find good evidence to support the answer.
   993→No relevant facts exist: Are Mayan masks modeled on space aliens?
   994→The question is based on preference or taste: Are Balinese or Mayan masks more
   995→beautiful?
   996→You must read too many sources: How are masks made? You don't want to plow
   997→through countless reports to find the best evidence (this usually results from a question
   998→that's too broad).
   999→You can't get the sources that your readers think are crucial. In even moderately
  1000→advanced projects, you'll be expected to work with the best sources available; for a
  1001→thesis and dissertation, they're essential. If you can't obtain those sources, find another
  1002→question.
  1003→3. You can't plausibly disprove the answer.
  1004→The answer seems self-evident because the evidence overwhelmingly favors one
  1005→answer. How important are masks in Inuit culture? The answer is obvious: Very. If you
  1006→can't imagine disproving a claim, then proving it is pointless. (On the other hand, worldclass reputations have been won by those who questioned a claim that seemed selfevidently true—for instance, that the sun went around the earth—and dared to disprove
  1007→it.)
  1008→
  1009→www.itpub.net
  1010→
  1011→Don't reject a question because you think someone must already have asked it. Until you
  1012→know, pursue its answer as if you asked first. Even if someone has answered it, you might
  1013→come up with a better answer or at least one with a new slant. In fact, in the humanities and
  1014→social sciences the best questions usually have more than one good answer. You can also
  1015→organize your project around comparing and contrasting competing answers and supporting
  1016→the best one (see 6.2.5).
  1017→The point is to find a question that you want to answer. Too many students, both graduate
  1018→and undergraduate, think that the aim of education is to memorize settled answers to someone
  1019→else's questions. It is not. It is to learn to find your own answers to your own questions. To do
  1020→that, you must learn to wonder about things, to let them puzzle you, particularly about things
  1021→that seem most commonplace.

  1023→2.2 Propose Some Working Answers
  1024→Before you get deep into your project, try one more step. It is one that some beginners resist
  1025→but that experienced researchers usually attempt. Once you have a question, imagine some
  1026→plausible answers, no matter how sketchy or speculative. At this stage, don't worry whether
  1027→they're right. That comes later.
  1028→For example, suppose you ask, Why do some religions use masks in ceremonies while
  1029→others don't? You might speculate,
  1030→Maybe cultures with many spirits need masks to distinguish them.
  1031→Maybe masks are common in cultures that mix religion and medicine.
  1032→Maybe religions originating in the Middle East were influenced by the Jewish prohibition against idolatry.
  1033→
  1034→Even a general answer can suggest something worth studying:
  1035→Maybe it has to do with the role of masks in nonreligious areas of a culture.
  1036→
  1037→Try to imagine at least one plausible answer, no matter how tentative or speculative. If after
  1038→lots of research you can't confirm it, you can organize your report around why that answer
  1039→seemed reasonable at the time but turned out to be wrong, and so isn't worth the time of other
  1040→researchers. That in itself can be a valuable contribution to the conversation on your topic.
  1041→(See 10.1.1–10.1.2 for how to use an apparently good idea that turns out to be wrong.)
  1042→In fact, look for two or three plausible answers. Even if you prefer one, you can improve it
  1043→by testing it against the others, and in any event, you can't show that an answer is right if you
  1044→can't also show why others are wrong. Even early in the project, write out your answers as
  1045→clearly and as fully as you can. It is too easy to think that you have a clear idea when you
  1046→don't. Putting a foggy idea into words is the best way to clarify it, or to discover that you can't.
  1047→2.2.1 Decide on a Working Hypothesis
  1048→If one answer seems promising, call it your working hypothesis and use it to guide your
  1049→
  1050→research. You can, of course, look for evidence with no more than a question to guide you,
  1051→because any question limits the number of plausible answers. But even the most tentative
  1052→working hypothesis helps you to think ahead, especially about the kind of evidence that you'll
  1053→need to support it. Will you need numbers? quotations? observations? images? historical
  1054→facts? More important, what kind of evidence would disprove your hypothesis? Answer those
  1055→questions, and you know the kind of data to watch for and to keep. In fact, until you have a
  1056→hypothesis, you can't know whether any data you collect are relevant to any question worth
  1057→asking.
  1058→If you can't imagine any working hypothesis, reconsider your question. Review your list of
  1059→exploratory questions to find one that you can answer; if you skipped that step, go back to
  1060→2.1.3. You may even decide to start over with a new topic. That costs time in the short run,
  1061→but it may save you from a failed project. If you're working on a thesis or dissertation, you can
  1062→wait longer to firm up a hypothesis while you read and ponder, but don't get deeply into your
  1063→project without at least the glimmer of a possible answer.
  1064→Under no circumstances put off thinking about a hypothesis until you begin drafting your
  1065→report or, worse, until you've almost finished it. You might not settle on the best answer to
  1066→your question until you've written your last page: writing, even revising, is itself an act of
  1067→discovery. Just don't wait until that last page to start thinking about some answer.
  1068→2.2.2 Beware the Risks in a Working Hypothesis
  1069→It is a bad idea to settle on a final answer too soon. But many new researchers and some
  1070→experienced ones are afraid to consider any working hypothesis early in their project, even
  1071→one they hold lightly, because they fear it might bias their thinking. There is some risk of that,
  1072→but a working hypothesis need not close your mind to a better one. Even the most objective
  1073→scientist devises an experiment to test for just a few predicted outcomes, often just one. In
  1074→fact, researchers who don't state a hypothesis usually have one in mind, but don't want to seem
  1075→publicly committed to it, lest it turn out wrong.
  1076→A working hypothesis is a risk only if it blinds you to a better one or if you can't give it up
  1077→when the evidence says you should. So as in all relationships, don't fall too hard for your first
  1078→hypothesis: the more you like it, the less easily you'll see its flaws. Despite that risk, it's better
  1079→to start with a flawed hypothesis than with none at all.
  1080→2.2.3 If You Can't Find an Answer, Argue for Your Question
  1081→We have focused on questions so much that you might think that your project fails if you can't
  1082→answer yours. In fact, much important research explains why a question no one has asked
  1083→should be, even though the researcher can't answer it: Do turtles dream? Why is yawning
  1084→contagious but being sleepy isn't? Or is it? Such reports focus on why the question is
  1085→important and what a good answer might look like. Or you may find that someone has
  1086→answered your question, but incompletely or even, if you're lucky, incorrectly. If you can't
  1087→find the right answer, you help readers by showing that a widely accepted one is wrong. (See
  1088→10.1.2 for how to use this plan in your introduction.)
  1089→
  1090→www.itpub.net
  1091→
  1092→Only when you ask question after question will you develop the critical imagination you'll
  1093→need in any profession you follow. In fact, as experienced researchers know, most issues have
  1094→few, if any, final answers, because there are no final questions. They know that it's as
  1095→important to ask a new question as it is to answer an old one, and that one day their new
  1096→question will become old and yield to a newer researcher's still newer one.
  1097→Your job is to become that newer researcher.

  1099→2.3 Build a Storyboard to Plan and Guide Your Work
  1100→For a short paper, you might not need a detailed plan—a sketch of an outline might do. But
  1101→for a long project, you'll usually need more, especially for one as long as a thesis or
  1102→dissertation. The first plan that comes to mind is usually an outline, with its I's and II's and A's
  1103→and B's and so on (see 23.4.2). If you prefer an outline, use one, especially if your project is
  1104→relatively short. The problem is that an outline can force you to specify too much too soon and
  1105→so lock up a final form before you've done your best thinking.
  1106→To avoid that risk, many researchers, including those outside the academic world, plan long
  1107→reports on a storyboard. A storyboard is like an outline spread over several pages, with lots of
  1108→space for adding data and ideas as you go. It is more flexible than an outline: it can help you
  1109→plan your search for evidence, organize your argument, write a first draft, and test a final one.
  1110→As opposed to lines in an outline, you can physically move storyboard pages around without
  1111→having to print a new plan every time you try out a new organization. You can spread its
  1112→pages across a wall, group related pages, and put minor sections below major ones to create a
  1113→“picture” of your project that shows you at a glance the design of the whole and your
  1114→progress through it.
  1115→2.3.1 State Your Question and Working Hypotheses
  1116→To start a storyboard, state at the top of its first page your question and working hypothesis as
  1117→exactly as you can. Then add plausible alternatives to help you see more clearly its limits and
  1118→strengths. Add new hypotheses as you think of them, and cross off those you prove wrong.
  1119→But save them, because you might be able to use one of them in your introduction (see
  1120→10.1.1).
  1121→2.3.2 State Your Reasons
  1122→Put at the top of separate pages each reason that might support your best hypothesis, even if
  1123→you have only one or two (for more on reasons, see 5.4.2). Imagine explaining your project to
  1124→a friend. You say, I want to show that Alamo stories helped develop a unique Texan identity,
  1125→and your friend asks, Why do you think so? Your reasons are the general statements that you
  1126→offer to support your answer: Well, first, the stories distorted facts to emphasize what became
  1127→central to Texan identity; second, the stories were first used to show that Texas (and the Wild
  1128→West) was a new kind of frontier; third, . . . and so on.
  1129→
  1130→If you can think of only one or two reasons (you'll usually need more), put placeholders at
  1131→the tops of pages: Reason 3: Something about Alamo stories making Texans feel special. If
  1132→you know only how you want a reason to support your answer, state that: Reason 4:
  1133→Something to show that Alamo stories were more than just myth. Each reason, of course,
  1134→needs support, so for each reason, ask Why do I think that? What evidence will I need to prove
  1135→it? That will help you focus your search for evidence (see 2.3.3 and 5.4.2).
  1136→If you're new to your topic or early in your project, your reasons may be only educated
  1137→guesses that you'll change; if you don't, you might not be self-critical enough. But a list of
  1138→reasons, no matter how speculative, is the best framework to guide your research and focus
  1139→your thinking, and certainly better than no reasons at all.
  1140→2.3.3 Sketch in the Kind of Evidence You Should Look For
  1141→Every field prefers its own kinds of evidence—numbers, quotations, observations, historical
  1142→facts, images, and so on. So for each reason, sketch the kind of evidence that you think you'll
  1143→need to support it. Even imagine what the most convincing evidence would look like. If you
  1144→can't imagine the kind of evidence you'll need, leave that part of the page blank, then read
  1145→secondary sources to find out the kind of evidence that researchers in your field favor (see
  1146→3.1.2).
  1147→2.3.4 Look at the Whole
  1148→Lay the pages on a table or tape them on a wall. Then step back and look at their order. When
  1149→you plan a first draft, you must put its parts in some order, so you might as well think about
  1150→one now. Can you see a logic in your order? cause and effect? narrative time? relative
  1151→importance? complexity? length? (See 6.2.5 for more principles of order.) Try out different
  1152→orders. This storyboard isn't your final plan; it's only a tool to guide your thinking and
  1153→organize what you find.
  1154→When you fill a page, try drafting that section, because writing out your ideas can improve
  1155→your thinking at every stage of your project.
  1156→Someday, you may have the leisure to amble through sources, reading just what interests
  1157→you. Such random browsing has opened up important lines of research. But if your report is
  1158→due in a month or so, you can't wait for lightning to strike; you need a plan. A storyboard is a
  1159→simple and reliable device to help you create one.
  1160→
  1161→2.4 Organize a Writing Support Group
  1162→A down side of scholarly research is its isolation. Except for group projects, you'll read, think,
  1163→and write mostly alone. But it doesn't have to be that way, at least not entirely. Look for
  1164→someone other than your instructor or advisor who will talk with you about your progress,
  1165→review your drafts, even pester you about how much you have written. That might be a
  1166→generous friend, but look first for another writer so that you can comment on each other's
  1167→
  1168→www.itpub.net
  1169→
  1170→ideas and drafts.
  1171→Better yet is a writing group of four or five people working on their own projects who meet
  1172→regularly to discuss one another's work. Early on, start each meeting with a summary of each
  1173→person's project in that three-part sentence: I'm working on the topic of X, because I want to
  1174→find out Y, so that I (and you) can better understand Z. As your projects develop, start with an
  1175→“elevator story,” a short summary of your research that you might give someone in the
  1176→elevator on the way to the meeting. It should include that three-part sentence, a working
  1177→hypothesis, and the major reasons supporting it (see 13.4).
  1178→In later stages, the group shares outlines and drafts so that they can serve as surrogate
  1179→readers to anticipate how your final readers will respond. If your group has a problem with
  1180→your draft, so will your final readers. They can even help you brainstorm when you bog down.
  1181→But for most writers, a writing group is most valuable for the discipline it imposes. It is easier
  1182→to meet a schedule when you know you must report your progress to others.
  1183→Writing groups are standard practice for theses or dissertations. But the rules may differ for
  1184→a class paper. Some teachers think that a group or writing partner might provide more help
  1185→than is appropriate, so be clear with your instructor what your group will do. If you don't, she
  1186→may decide the assistance you have received is inappropriate (see 7.10).

  1273→Once you have at least a question and perhaps a working hypothesis along with a few
  1274→tentative reasons for supporting it, you can start looking for the data you'll need to support
  1275→your reasons and test your hypothesis. In this chapter we explain how to find those data and in
  1276→the next how to work with them. But don't think of finding sources and reading them as
  1277→separate steps. Once you have a promising source, read it to find other sources. And as you
  1278→fill your storyboard with notes, you'll discover gaps and new questions that only more sources

  1282→3.1 Understand the Kinds of Sources Readers Expect You to Use
  1283→
  1284→www.itpub.net
  1285→
  1286→Depending on your experience, readers will expect you to use different levels of sources,
  1287→called primary, secondary, and tertiary (think first-, second-, and thirdhand). These aren't
  1288→sharply defined categories, but they roughly characterize how researchers think about most
  1289→sources.
  1290→3.1.1 Consult Primary Sources for Evidence
  1291→In fields such as literary studies, the arts, and history, primary sources are original works—
  1292→diaries, letters, manuscripts, images, films, film scripts, recordings, and musical scores created
  1293→by writers, artists, composers, and so on. Those sources provide data—the words, images, and
  1294→sounds that you use as evidence to support your reasons. Data can also be objects: coins,
  1295→clothing, tools, and other artifacts from the period or belonging to a person you're studying.
  1296→In fields such as economics, psychology, chemistry, and so on, researchers typically collect
  1297→data through observation and experiment. In others, researchers gather evidence through
  1298→interviews. (To conduct effective interviews, you must use reliable methods for eliciting and
  1299→recording the information you collect.) In such fields, evidence consists of the data that
  1300→researchers collect. The primary sources for those collected data are the publications that first
  1301→publish them, ranging from government and commercial databases to scholarly journals.
  1302→Experienced researchers look for data in primary sources first. If, for example, you were
  1303→writing on Alamo stories, you'd try to find sources written at the time—letters, diaries,
  1304→eyewitness reports, and so on.
  1305→3.1.2 Read Secondary Sources to Learn from Other Researchers
  1306→Secondary sources are books and articles that analyze primary sources, usually written by and
  1307→for other researchers. A report in a scholarly journal analyzing Alamo stories would be a
  1308→secondary source for researchers working on those stories. Secondary sources also include
  1309→specialized encyclopedias and dictionaries that offer essays written by scholars in a field. You
  1310→use secondary sources for three purposes:
  1311→1. To Keep Up with Current Research. Researchers read secondary sources to keep up with
  1312→the work of other researchers, to inform and refine their thinking, and to motivate their
  1313→own work by adding to a published line of research.
  1314→2. To Find Other Points of View. A research report is not complete until the researcher
  1315→acknowledges and responds to the views of others and to his readers' predictable questions
  1316→and disagreements (see 5.4.3). You can find most of those other points of view in
  1317→secondary sources. What alternatives to your ideas do they offer? What evidence do they
  1318→cite that you must acknowledge? Some new researchers think they weaken their case if
  1319→they mention any view opposing their own. The truth is the opposite. When you
  1320→acknowledge competing views, you show readers that you not only know those views but
  1321→can confidently respond to them (for more on this, see 5.4.3).
  1322→More important, you must use those competing views to improve your own. You can't
  1323→
  1324→understand what you think until you understand why a rational person might think
  1325→differently. So as you look for sources, don't look just for those that support your views. Be
  1326→alert as well for those that contradict them.
  1327→3. To Find Models for Your Own Research and Analysis. You can use secondary sources
  1328→to find out not just what others have written about your topic, but how they have written
  1329→about it, as models for the form and style of your own report. Imagine a secondary source
  1330→as a colleague talking to you about your topic. As you respond, you'd want to sound like
  1331→someone who knows the field, and so you'd try to learn how she reasons, the language she
  1332→uses, the kinds of evidence she offers, and the kinds she rarely or never uses. The
  1333→“conversation” would be in writing, so you'd even imitate stylistic details such as whether
  1334→she writes in long paragraphs or breaks up her pages with subheads and bullet points
  1335→(common in the social sciences, less common in the humanities).
  1336→You can also use a secondary source as a model for your conceptual analysis. If, for
  1337→example, you were analyzing Alamo stories, you might study how a source treats Custer's
  1338→Last Stand. Is its approach psychological, social, historical, political? Its particular reasons or
  1339→evidence will probably be irrelevant to your project, but you might support your answer with
  1340→the same kinds of data and reasoning, perhaps even following the same organization.
  1341→So if you come across a source that's not exactly on your topic but treats one like it, skim it
  1342→to see how that researcher thinks about his material and presents it. (You don't have to cite
  1343→that source if you use only its general logic, but you may cite it to give your own approach
  1344→more authority.)
  1345→Researchers use data reported in secondary sources only when they can't find them in
  1346→primary sources. Then they're cautious about using those secondary sources, because
  1347→secondhand reports of data have a high error rate. If you're doing very advanced work, check
  1348→the accuracy of important quotations, facts, or numbers from secondary sources. Those who
  1349→publish in respected places rarely misreport deliberately, but they make careless mistakes
  1350→more often than nonexperts think or experts admit.
  1351→Of course, if you were studying how the Alamo story has been analyzed, then secondary
  1352→sources offering those analyses would be your primary sources.
  1353→If you're new to a field, you may find secondary sources hard to read: they assume a lot of
  1354→background knowledge, and many aren't clearly written (see 11.2). If you're working on a
  1355→topic new to you, you might begin with an overview in a specialized encyclopedia or reliable
  1356→tertiary source.
  1357→3.1.3 Read Tertiary Sources for Introductory Overviews
  1358→Tertiary sources are based on secondary sources, usually written for non-specialists. They
  1359→include general encyclopedias and dictionaries, as well as newspapers and magazines like
  1360→Time and the Atlantic Monthly and commercial books written for a general audience. Welledited general encyclopedias offer a quick overview of many topics. Beware, however, of
  1361→online encyclopedias, such as Wikipedia, that rely on anonymous contributions rather than on
  1362→carefully edited entries written by established researchers. Wikipedia has proved to be
  1363→
  1364→www.itpub.net
  1365→
  1366→relatively accurate in the sciences, but overall it is uneven and sometimes wrong. Never cite it
  1367→as an authoritative source.
  1368→Be similarly cautious about using magazine and newspaper articles. Some describe research
  1369→reported in secondary sources reliably, but most oversimplify, or worse, misreport it. You
  1370→would, of course, treat such a source as primary if you were studying how it deals with a
  1371→topic, such as gender bias in the Encyclopedia Britannica or hoaxes in Wikipedia.
  1372→Once you understand kinds of sources, you can begin looking for them.

  1374→3.2 Record Your Sources Fully, Accurately, and Appropriately
  1375→Before you look for sources, you should know how to cite the ones you find. Your readers
  1376→will trust your report only if they trust your evidence, and they won't trust your evidence if
  1377→they can't find your sources. Your first obligation as a researcher is to cite your sources
  1378→accurately and fully so that your readers can find them.
  1379→3.2.1 Determine Your Citation Style
  1380→Most fields require a specific citation style. The two most common ones are described in
  1381→detail in part 2:
  1382→notes-bibliography style (or simply bibliography style), used widely in the humanities and
  1383→in some social sciences (see chapters 16 and 17)
  1384→parenthetical citations–reference list style (or simply reference list style), used in most
  1385→social sciences and in the natural sciences (see chapters 18 and 19)
  1386→If you are uncertain which style to use, consult your instructor. Before you start compiling
  1387→your list of sources, read the general introduction to citations in chapter 15, then depending on
  1388→the citation style you are required to use, read the introduction to bibliography style (chapter
  1389→16) or reference list style (chapter 18).
  1390→3.2.2 Record Bibliographic Data
  1391→To save time and avoid errors, record all the citation information you will need when you first
  1392→find a source. Most of this information appears on the title page of a book or at the head of a
  1393→journal article. The specific information you need depends on the type of source, but for each
  1394→source, record at least the following:
  1395→Who wrote or assembled the source?
  1396→author(s)
  1397→editor(s)

  1428→3.3 Search for Sources Systematically
  1429→As you search for sources, you must be knowledgeably systematic, because if you miss an
  1430→
  1431→www.itpub.net
  1432→
  1433→important one, you'll lose credibility.
  1434→3.3.1 Look for Someone Who Knows Something about Your Topic
  1435→You might start by asking around to find someone who knows something about your topic
  1436→and standard reference works on it: advanced students, faculty, even people outside the
  1437→academic community. You might look up your topic in the yellow pages of the phonebook.
  1438→You won't always find someone, but you might get lucky.
  1439→3.3.2 Skim the Internet
  1440→Before college, many students do research only on the Web, because their high school
  1441→libraries are small and they need to find only a few sources. In college, you can still do some
  1442→preliminary work with a scholarly search engine such as Google Scholar. It will give you a
  1443→rough idea of the kinds of sources available. If your library catalog is online, you can also
  1444→start there (see 3.3.6 below). But if you search just the Internet, you'll miss important sources
  1445→that you'll find only by poking around in your library. Once again, you'll work most
  1446→efficiently if you have a plan.
  1447→3.3.3 Talk to Reference Librarians
  1448→If you don't know how to find what you need, ask a librarian. Most college libraries offer
  1449→tours of reference rooms and special collections, and short seminars on how to search the
  1450→catalog, databases, and other sources of information. If you're a new researcher, seize every
  1451→opportunity to learn online search techniques in your field.
  1452→You can also talk to librarians who specialize in your area. They won't find sources for you,
  1453→but they will help you look for them. If you have a research question, share it: I'm looking for
  1454→data on X because I want to find out. . . . If you have a working hypothesis and reasons, share
  1455→them too: I'm looking for data to show Y [your reason] because I want to claim Z [your
  1456→hypothesis]. Rehearse your questions to avoid wasting your time and theirs.
  1457→3.3.4 Browse in Your Reference Area
  1458→Researchers in all fields share common values and habits of thought, but every field has its
  1459→own ways of doing things. To learn about the ways of your field, browse the shelves in your
  1460→library's reference room that hold guides to your field's particular research methods,
  1461→databases, and special resources (in the bibliography, see items in category 3 in your field). At
  1462→least familiarize yourself with the following resources (in the bibliography, see category 4 for
  1463→lists of sources in your field; many are also online):
  1464→a bibliography of works published each year in your field, such as Philosopher's Index or
  1465→Education Index
  1466→summary bibliographies of works on a specific topic collected over several years

  1469→collections of abstracts that summarize articles in newspapers and in professional
  1470→journals
  1471→reviews of the year's work; look for a title in your field beginning with Reviews in . ..
  1472→for new fields, Web sites maintained by individuals or scholarly associations

  1515→3.3.8 Browse the Shelves
  1516→You might think that online research is faster than walking around your library. But it can be
  1517→slower, and if you work only online, you may miss crucial sources that you'll find only in the
  1518→library. More important, you'll miss the benefits of serendipity—a chance encounter with a
  1519→source that you find only in person.
  1520→If you're allowed in the stacks (where all the books that you can check out are kept), find
  1521→the shelf with books on your topic. Then scan the titles on that shelf and the ones above,
  1522→below, and on either side. Then turn around and skim titles behind you; you never know.
  1523→When you spot a promising title, especially on a university press book with a new binding,
  1524→skim its table of contents, then its index for keywords related to your question or its answer.
  1525→Then skim its bibliography for titles that look relevant to your project. You can do all that
  1526→faster with books on a shelf than you can online.
  1527→If the book looks promising, skim its preface or introduction. If it still looks promising, set
  1528→it aside for a closer look. Even if it doesn't seem relevant, record its Library of Congress call
  1529→number and bibliographic data (author, title, publisher, date of publication and so on; see part
  1530→2 of this manual for the details), and in a few words summarize what the book seems to be
  1531→about. A month later, you might realize that it's more useful than you thought.
  1532→You can check tables of contents for many journals online, but browsing in the journals
  1533→area of a library can be more productive. Find the journals that have promising articles. Skim
  1534→tables of contents for the prior ten years. Most volumes include a yearly summary table of
  1535→contents. Then take a quick look at the journals shelved nearby. Skim their most recent tables
  1536→of contents. You will be surprised at how often you find a relevant article that you would have
  1537→missed had you done your work entirely online.
  1538→If you are new to a field, you can get a rough impression of the academic quality of a
  1539→journal by its look. If it's on glossy paper with lots of illustrations, even advertisements, it
  1540→might be more journalistic than scholarly. Those are not infallible signs of unreliable
  1541→scholarship, but they are worth considering.

  1554→3.4 Evaluate Sources for Relevance and Reliability
  1555→You will probably find more sources than you can use, so you must evaluate their usefulness
  1556→by skimming quickly for two criteria: relevance and reliability.
  1557→3.4.1 Evaluate the Relevance of Sources
  1558→Once you decide a book might be relevant, skim it systematically:
  1559→If its index lists keywords related to your question or its answers, skim the pages on
  1560→which those words occur.
  1561→Skim its introduction, especially its last page, where writers often outline their text.
  1562→Skim its last chapter, especially the first and last six or seven pages.
  1563→If you have time, do the same for chapters that look relevant, especially those for which
  1564→the index lists many of your keywords.
  1565→If the source is a collection of articles, skim the editor's introduction.
  1566→(Be sure that you're looking at a book's most recent edition. Over time, researchers change
  1567→their views, refining them, even rejecting earlier ones.) If you're doing advanced work, read
  1568→book reviews of promising sources (see section 4 of the bibliography of resources in your
  1569→field).
  1570→If your source is a journal article, do this:
  1571→Read its abstract, if any.
  1572→Skim the last two or three paragraphs of the introduction (or other opening section) and
  1573→all of any section called “Conclusion.”
  1574→If the article has no separate introduction or conclusion, skim its first and last few
  1575→
  1576→www.itpub.net
  1577→
  1578→paragraphs.
  1579→Skim the first paragraph or two after each subhead, if any.
  1580→If your source is online, do this:
  1581→If it looks like a printed article, follow the steps for a journal article.
  1582→Skim any section labeled “Introduction,” “Overview,” “Summary,” or the like. If there is
  1583→none, look for a link labeled “About the Site” or something similar.
  1584→If the site has a link labeled “Site Map” or “Index,” follow it and check the list, looking
  1585→for keywords related to your question or its answers. Click to skim those pages.
  1586→If the site has a “search” resource, type in keywords from your topic.
  1587→3.4.2 Evaluate the Reliability of Print Sources
  1588→You can't judge a source until you read it, but there are signs of its reliability:
  1589→1. Is the author a reputable scholar? Most publications cite an author's academic
  1590→credentials; you can find more with a search engine. Most established scholars are reliable,
  1591→but be cautious if the topic is a contested social issue such as gun control or abortion. Even
  1592→reputable scholars can have axes to grind, especially if their research is supported by a
  1593→special interest group.
  1594→2. Is the source current? Many reputable scholars write books and articles popularizing the
  1595→research of others. But by the time you read them, these tertiary sources may be out of
  1596→date. How fast a source dates varies by subject, so check with someone who knows your
  1597→field. For journal articles in the social sciences, more than ten years is pushing the limit.
  1598→For books, figure fifteen or so. Publications in the humanities have a longer life span.
  1599→3. Is the source published by a reputable press? You can trust most university presses,
  1600→especially those at well-known schools. Before they publish a manuscript, they ask experts
  1601→to review it (a process called peer review). You can also trust some commercial presses in
  1602→some fields, such as Norton in literature, Ablex in sciences, or West in the law. Be
  1603→skeptical of a commercial book that makes sensational claims, even if its author has a PhD
  1604→after his name.
  1605→4. Was the article peer-reviewed? Most scholarly journals, both print and online, publish
  1606→only peer-reviewed articles. Few commercial magazines use peer review, and fewer still
  1607→check an author's facts. If a report hasn't been peer-reviewed, use it cautiously.
  1608→5. Has the source received good reviews? If the source is a book published more than a
  1609→year ago, it may have been reviewed in a journal in the field. Many fields have indexes to
  1610→published reviews that tell you how others evaluate a source. (See the bibliography.)
  1611→6. Has the source been frequently cited by others? You can roughly estimate how
  1612→
  1613→influential a source is by how often others cite it. To determine that, consult a citation
  1614→index (in the bibliography, see section 4 in your field).
  1615→Those signs don't guarantee that a source is reliable, but they should give you reasonable
  1616→confidence in it. If you can't find reliable sources, acknowledge the limits of the ones you
  1617→have. Of course, you may find an exciting research problem when you discover that a source
  1618→thought to be reliable is not.
  1619→3.4.3 Evaluate the Reliability of Online Sources
  1620→Evaluate online sources as you do those in print, but more cautiously. The number of reliable
  1621→Web sources grows every day, but they are still islands in a swamp of misinformation. If you
  1622→find data available only on the Web, look for sites or online publications with these signs of
  1623→reliability:
  1624→1. The site is sponsored by a reputable organization. Some sites supported by individuals
  1625→are reliable; most are not.
  1626→2. It is related to a reliable professional journal.
  1627→3. It supplements reliable print sources. Some journals use the Web to host discussions
  1628→among authors and readers, to offer data too new to be in libraries, to archive data not in
  1629→articles, or to present illustrations too expensive to print. Many government and academic
  1630→databases are only online.
  1631→4. It avoids heated advocacy for or against a contested social issue.
  1632→5. It does not make wild claims, attack other researchers, use abusive language, or make
  1633→errors of spelling, punctuation, and grammar.
  1634→6. It indicates when the site was last updated. If it has no date, be cautious.
  1635→Trust a site only if careful readers would trust those who maintain it. If you don't know who
  1636→maintains it, be skeptical.
  1637→Online services now provide reliable editions of many older texts. You'll also find welledited texts at many university sites. It's “one-stop shopping”; you never have to move from
  1638→your chair. Online services are, however, far less complete than most university libraries, and
  1639→using them will teach you nothing about doing research in a real library. Some day,
  1640→everything ever printed will be available online (a future that gives some researchers mixed
  1641→feelings). But until then, surfing the Web doesn't replace prowling the stacks.

  1644→If you are writing a class paper, you'll usually have to focus narrowly on the kinds of sources
  1645→typically used in your field. But if you are doing an advanced project such as an MA thesis or
  1646→PhD dissertation, find an opportunity to search beyond them. If, for example, you were doing
  1647→
  1648→www.itpub.net
  1649→
  1650→a project on the economic effects of agricultural changes on London grain markets in 1600,
  1651→you might read some Elizabethan plays, look at pictures of working-class life, or look for

  1653→visual representations of daily life in London, you might work up the economic history of the
  1654→period and place. You can't do this in the limited time you have for short papers, but when
  1655→you have months to work on a major project, try to look beyond the standard kinds of
  1656→references relevant to your question. When you do, you enrich not only your specific analysis
  1657→but your range of intellectual reference and your ability to synthesize diverse kinds of data, a
  1658→crucial competence of an inquiring mind.

  1735→4.1 Read Generously to Understand, Then Critically to Engage and Evaluate
  1736→For an advanced project, take the time to read your most promising sources twice, first
  1737→quickly and generously to understand them on their own terms. If you disagree too soon, you
  1738→can misunderstand or exaggerate a weakness.
  1739→Then reread them slowly and critically, as if you were amiably but pointedly questioning a
  1740→friend; imagine his or her answers, then question them. If you disagree, don't just reject a
  1741→source: read it in ways that will encourage your own original thinking.
  1742→You probably won't be able to engage your sources fully until after you've done some
  1743→reading and developed a few ideas of your own. But from the outset, be alert for ways to read
  1744→your sources not passively, as a consumer, but actively and creatively, as an engaged partner.
  1745→At some point, better earlier than later, you must look for ways to go beyond your sources,
  1746→even when you agree with them.
  1747→4.1.1 Look for Creative Agreement
  1748→It is a happy moment when a source confirms your views. But if you just passively agree, you
  1749→won't develop any of your own ideas. So try to extend what your source claims: What new
  1750→cases might it cover? What new insights can it provide? Is there confirming evidence your
  1751→source hasn't considered? Here are some ways to agree creatively.
  1752→OFFER ADDITIONAL SUPPORT. You have new evidence to support a source's claim.
  1753→Smith uses anecdotal evidence to show that the Alamo story had mythic status beyond Texas, but a study of bigcity newspapers offers better evidence.
  1754→
  1755→www.itpub.net
  1756→
  1757→1. Source supports a claim with old evidence, but maybe you can offer new evidence.
  1758→2. Source supports a claim with weak evidence, but maybe you can offer stronger
  1759→evidence.
  1760→CONFIRM UNSUPPORTED CLAIMS. You can prove something that a source has only assumed
  1761→
  1762→or speculated.
  1763→
  1764→Smith recommends visualization to improve sports performance, but a study of the mental activities of athletes
  1765→shows why that is good advice.
  1766→
  1767→1. Source only speculates that X might be true, but maybe you can offer evidence to show
  1768→that it definitely is.
  1769→2. Source assumes that X is true, but maybe you can prove it.
  1770→APPLY A CLAIM MORE WIDELY. You can extend a position to new areas.
  1771→Smith has shown that medical students learn physiological processes better when they are explained with many
  1772→metaphors rather than by just one. The same appears to be true for engineers learning physical processes.
  1773→
  1774→1. Source correctly applies his claim to one situation, but maybe it can apply to new ones.
  1775→2. Source claims that X is true in a specific situation, but maybe it's true in general.
  1776→4.1.2 Look for Creative Disagreement
  1777→It is even more important to note when you disagree with a source, because that might suggest
  1778→a working hypothesis for your whole report. So instead of just noting that you disagree with
  1779→its views, use that disagreement to encourage your own productive thinking. Here are some
  1780→kinds of disagreement (these aren't sharply defined categories; many overlap).
  1781→CONTRADICTIONS OF KIND. A source says something is one kind of thing, but maybe it's
  1782→
  1783→another kind.
  1784→
  1785→Smith says that certain religious groups are considered “cults” because of their strange beliefs, but those beliefs
  1786→are no different in kind from standard religions.
  1787→
  1788→1. Source claims that X is a kind of Y (or like it), but maybe it's not.
  1789→2. Source claims that X always has Y as one its features or qualities, but maybe it doesn't.
  1790→3. Source claims that X is normal/good/significant/useful/moral/interesting/. . ., but maybe
  1791→it's not.
  1792→(You can reverse those claims and the ones that follow to state the opposite: though a source
  1793→says X is not a kind of Y, you can show that it is.)
  1794→
  1795→PART-WHOLE CONTRADICTIONS. You can show that a source mistakes how the parts of
  1796→
  1797→something are related.
  1798→
  1799→Smith has argued that sports are crucial to an educated person, but in fact athletics has no place in college.
  1800→
  1801→1. Source claims that X is a part of Y, but maybe it's not.
  1802→2. Source claims that part of X relates to another of its parts in a certain way, but maybe it
  1803→doesn't.
  1804→3. Source claims that every X has Y as one of its parts, but maybe it doesn't.
  1805→DEVELOPMENTAL OR HISTORICAL CONTRADICTIONS.
  1806→
  1807→mistakes the origin and development of a topic.
  1808→
  1809→You can show that a source
  1810→
  1811→Smith argues that the world population will continue to rise, but it will not.
  1812→
  1813→1. Source claims that X is changing, but maybe it's not.
  1814→2. Source claims that X originated in Y, but maybe it didn't.
  1815→3. Source claims that X develops in a certain way, but maybe it doesn't.
  1816→EXTERNAL CAUSE-EFFECT CONTRADICTIONS. You can show that a source mistakes a
  1817→
  1818→causal relationship:
  1819→
  1820→Smith claims that juveniles can be stopped from becoming criminals by “boot camps.” But evidence shows that
  1821→it makes them more likely to become criminals.
  1822→
  1823→1. Source claims that X causes Y, but maybe it doesn't.
  1824→2. Source claims that X causes Y, but maybe they are both caused by Z.
  1825→3. Source claims that X is sufficient to cause Y, but maybe it's not.
  1826→4. Source claims that X causes only Y, but maybe it also causes Z.
  1827→Most contradictions don't change a conceptual
  1828→framework, but when you can contradict a standard view of things, you urge others to think in
  1829→a new way.
  1830→CONTRADICTIONS OF PERSPECTIVE.
  1831→
  1832→Smith assumes that advertising is a purely economic function, but it also serves as a laboratory for new art forms.
  1833→
  1834→1. Source discusses X in the context of or from the point of view of Y, but maybe a new
  1835→context or point of view reveals a new truth (the new or old context can be social, political,
  1836→philosophical, historical, economic, ethical, gender specific, etc.).
  1837→2. Source analyzes X using theory/value system Y, but maybe you can analyze X from a
  1838→new point of view and see it in a new way.
  1839→
  1840→www.itpub.net
  1841→
  1842→As we said, you probably won't be able to engage sources in these ways until after you've
  1843→read enough to form some views of your own. But if you keep these ways of thinking in mind
  1844→as you begin to read, you'll engage your sources sooner and more productively.
  1845→Of course, once you discover that you can productively agree or disagree with a source, you
  1846→should then ask So what? So what if you can show that while Smith claims that Easterners did
  1847→not embrace the story of the Alamo enthusiastically, in fact many did?

  1852→4.2.1 Create Templates for Notes
  1853→You will take notes more reliably if you set up a system that encourages you to think beyond
  1854→the mere content of your sources by analyzing and organizing that content into useful
  1855→categories. A few instructors still recommend taking notes in longhand on 3 × 5 cards, as in
  1856→figure 4.1. A card like that may seem old-fashioned, but it provides a template for efficient
  1857→note-taking, even if you take notes on a laptop. (Start a new page for each general idea or
  1858→claim that you record from a source.) Here is a plan for such a template:
  1859→At the top of each new page, create a space for bibliographic data (author, short title,
  1860→page number).
  1861→Create another space at the top for keywords (see upper right above). Those words will
  1862→later will let you sort and re-sort your notes by subject matter (for more on keywords, see
  1863→4.3.4).
  1864→Create different places on each new page for different kinds of notes. You might even
  1865→
  1866→label the places (see fig. 4.1, with places for a Claim, Data, and My Qs).
  1867→In particular, create a section specifically dedicated to your own responses, agreements,
  1868→disagreements, speculations, and so on. That will encourage you to do more than simply
  1869→record the content of what you read.
  1870→When you quote the words of a source, record them in a distinctive color or font size and
  1871→style so that you can recognize quotations at a glance, and enclose them in large quotation
  1872→marks in case the file loses its formatting.
  1873→When you paraphrase a passage (see 4.2.2), record the paraphrase in a distinctive color or
  1874→font so that you can't possibly mistake it for your own ideas, and enclose it in curly
  1875→brackets (in case the file loses its formatting).
  1876→If you can't take notes directly on a computer, make paper copies of the template.
  1877→4.2.2 Know When to Summarize, Paraphrase, or Quote
  1878→It would take you forever to transcribe the exact words of every source you might want to use,
  1879→so you must know when not to quote, but to summarize or paraphrase.
  1880→Summarize when you need only the general point of a passage, section, or even whole
  1881→article or book. Summary is useful for general context or related but not specifically relevant
  1882→data or views. A summary of a source never serves as good evidence (see 5.4.2 for more on
  1883→evidence).
  1884→Paraphrase when you can represent what a source says more clearly or pointedly than it
  1885→does. Paraphrase doesn't mean just changing a word or two. You must use your own words
  1886→and your own phrasing to replace most of the words and phrasing of the passage (see 7.9.2). A
  1887→paraphrase is never as good evidence as a direct quotation.
  1888→Record exact quotations when they serve these purposes:
  1889→The quoted words constitute evidence that backs up your reasons. If, for example, you
  1890→wanted to claim that different regions responded to the Battle of the Alamo differently, you
  1891→would quote exact words from different newspapers. You would paraphrase them if you
  1892→needed only their general sentiments.
  1893→The words are from an authority who backs up your view.
  1894→They are strikingly original.
  1895→They express your ideas so compellingly that the quotation can frame the rest of your
  1896→discussion.
  1897→They state a view that you disagree with, and to be fair you want to state that view
  1898→exactly.

  1907→4.2.3 Guard against Inadvertent Plagiarism
  1908→Sloppy note-taking has caused grief for students and professionals alike, ranging from ridicule
  1909→for trivial errors to professional exile for inadvertent plagiarism. To avoid that risk, commit to
  1910→heart these two iron rules for recording information in notes:
  1911→Always unambiguously identify words and ideas from a source so that weeks or months
  1912→later you cannot possibly mistake them for your own. As recommended above, record
  1913→quotations and paraphrases with quotation marks, as well as in a font that unambiguously
  1914→distinguishes them from your own ideas.
  1915→Never paraphrase a source so closely that a reader can match the phrasing and sense of
  1916→your words with those in your source (see 7.9.2).
  1917→In fact, rather than retyping quotations of more than a few lines, download or photocopy
  1918→them. Add to the top of the downloaded or photocopied page the name of the source and
  1919→keywords for sorting.
  1920→This is important: never assume that you can use what you find online without citing its
  1921→source, even if it's free and publicly available. Nothing releases you from the duty to
  1922→acknowledge your use of anything you did not personally create yourself. (For more on
  1923→plagiarism, see 7.9.)

  1925→4.3 Take Useful Notes
  1926→Readers will judge your report not just by the quality of your sources and how accurately you
  1927→report them, but also by how deeply you engage them. To do that, you must take notes in a
  1928→way that not only reflects but encourages a growing understanding of your project.
  1929→4.3.1 Use Note-Taking to Advance Your Thinking
  1930→Many inexperienced researchers think that note-taking is a matter of merely recording data.
  1931→Once they find a source, they download or photocopy pages or write down exactly what's on
  1932→them. Recording and photocopying can help you quote or paraphrase accurately, but if that's
  1933→all you do, if you don't engage your sources actively, you will simply accumulate a lot of inert
  1934→data that are likely to be equally inert in your report.
  1935→If you photocopy lots of text, annotate it in a way that engages your critical thinking. Start
  1936→by picking out those sentences that express crucial elements in a chapter or article (its claim,
  1937→
  1938→major reasons, and so on). Highlight or label them in the margin. Then mark ideas or data
  1939→that you expect to include in your report. (If you use a highlighter, use different colors to
  1940→indicate these different elements.)
  1941→Then on the back of the photocopied pages, summarize what you've highlighted or sketch a
  1942→response to it, or make notes in the margin that help you interpret the highlighting. The more
  1943→you write about a source now, the better you will understand and remember it later.
  1944→4.3.2 Take Notes Relevant to Your Question and Working Hypothesis
  1945→To make your notes most useful, record not just the facts that you think you can use as
  1946→evidence, but data that help you explain those facts and their relationship to your claim. You
  1947→can create a notes template to help you remember to look for several different kinds of
  1948→information (see 4.2.1).
  1949→The first three items are directly relevant to your working hypothesis:
  1950→reasons that support your hypothesis or suggest a new one
  1951→evidence that supports your reasons
  1952→views that undermine or even contradict your hypothesis
  1953→Do not limit your notes to supporting data. You will need to respond to data that qualify or
  1954→even contradict your hypothesis when you make your case in support of it (see 5.4.3).
  1955→These next items might not support or challenge your hypothesis, but they may help you
  1956→explain its context or simply make your report more readable:
  1957→historical background of your question, what authorities have said about it, particularly
  1958→earlier research (see 6.2.2 and 10.1.1)
  1959→historical or contemporary context that explains the importance of your question
  1960→important definitions and principles of analysis
  1961→analogies, comparisons, and anecdotes that might not directly support your hypothesis
  1962→but do explain or illustrate complicated issues or simply make your analysis more
  1963→interesting
  1964→strikingly original language relevant to your topic
