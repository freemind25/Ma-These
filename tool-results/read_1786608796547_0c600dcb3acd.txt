   347→Introduction
   348→
   349→In 2005, Routledge published the ﬁrst edition of A Handbook for Doctoral
   350→Supervisors written by Stan Taylor and the late Nigel Beasley, to whose memory
   351→this edition is dedicated. The book was well received and was widely used as a
   352→textbook and a source for professional development by supervisors across the
   353→globe. Over time, it became out of date and Routledge commissioned a second
   354→edition written by Stan Taylor, Margaret Kiley, and Robin Humphrey which was
   355→published in 2018.
   356→It is a measure of the pace of change in doctoral education that, six years later,
   357→there is a need for a further edition. This reﬂects the fact that, in the relatively
   358→short space of time since the second edition was published, there have been sig­
   359→niﬁcant developments. These include variously; a much stronger focus on ensuring
   360→equality of opportunity in access, particularly for indigenous populations and other
   361→ethnic and racial minorities; a greater emphasis on ‘respect’ in supervisory-candi­
   362→date relationships; a move towards online supervision and online examination; an
   363→increased realisation of the need to offer doctoral candidates appropriate personal
   364→support during their studies, particularly in relation to their wellbeing and mental
   365→health; and a heightened awareness of the importance of assisting them to prepare
   366→them for careers outside as well as inside academia.
   367→These developments pre-dated the Covid-19 outbreak of 2020–2022 but in
   368→many cases their importance was dramatically ampliﬁed during the pandemic,
   369→leaving a legacy that is still continuing. All of them have impacted the roles of
   370→supervisors, particularly in terms of recruiting and selecting candidates, managing
   371→working relationships, offering academic advice and support in the online envir­
   372→onment, and providing personal, professional, and career support. These changes
   373→have attracted a considerable amount of attention from researchers in the ﬁeld of
   374→doctoral education, and this has been reﬂected in a rapid growth in published
   375→research.
   376→The aims of this new edition are to incorporate these changes in the roles of
   377→supervisors into the content of the Handbook and to bring the latter up to date
   378→with the latest research. This has involved a substantial re-writing of many of the
   379→original chapters to incorporate new themes and new materials.

   388→version has then been co-authored by Stan Taylor and Margaret Kiley.
   389→As with the ﬁrst and second editions, the third is aimed at the potential or
   390→established supervisor/advisor facing the changing world of doctoral super­

   392→practice.
   393→We are aware that, while there is no hard and fast rule, the term ‘supervisor’
   394→tends to be used in most of Europe, the UK, and Oceania. Elsewhere, parti­
   395→cularly in the United States and countries with systems based upon that model,
   396→the more common term is ‘advisor’. We are also aware that this is not just a
   397→matter of style but also of substance; ‘supervisor’ implies a wider range of
   398→intervention with regard to the candidate and to the research project than
   399→‘advisor’. But, while we have written this book for supervisors (and used the
   400→term throughout) we believe that most, if not all, of it is also relevant to
   401→advisors, that is, in covering the roles of the former we are also covering those
   402→of the latter.
   403→With regard to ‘supervision’, it may be noted that our use of this term is pri­
   404→marily conﬁned to the supervision of the candidate’s research project. It does not
   405→include any taught components, but we do think it is important that, particularly
   406→in the case of more structured doctorates, supervisors are aware of the whole
   407→doctoral programme in order that they can, where appropriate, advise candidates
   408→how to build upon their previous studies.
   409→With regard to ‘effective’, this could be taken as meaning simply getting the
   410→candidate over the line to a doctorate. But we believe there is more to it than that
   411→in terms of both ‘outcomes’ and ‘outputs’. With regard to the former, ‘effective­
   412→ness’ means a high-quality submission, completion on time or as near as possible,
   413→and dissemination to the subject community; with regard to the latter, it means
   414→that the candidate has successfully developed the creative, critical, and analytical
   415→skills and knowledge of a researcher and laid the basis for their future career inside,
   416→or increasingly outside, academia.

   519→Introduction
   520→Supervisors tend to be immersed in their busy academic and/or professional lives,
   521→not to mention their personal ones, and seldom have the time to maintain an
   522→overview of ongoing developments and research in doctoral education. As a con­
   523→sequence, many are experiencing new demands without necessarily understanding
   524→the wider context within which they arise. The purpose of the present chapter is to
   525→try to set that wider context. It is based on the work of Taylor (2012, 2023) and
   526→begins with a brief summary of the historical origins and evolution of doctoral
   527→education from its modern origins through to the 1980s and then looks at sub­
   528→sequent developments that have transformed the ﬁeld and the context of
   529→supervision.
   530→
   531→The origins and evolution of doctoral education
   532→Doctorates have their origins in the mediaeval universities when they were used
   533→primarily as a means of licensing teachers in the professions of medicine, law, and
   534→theology (Bernstein et al. 2014, Amaral and Carvalho 2020). This paradigm
   535→remained dominant until, in the early 19th century, a number of eminent
   536→Enlightenment thinkers in Prussia began to advocate a new kind of university, one
   537→in which the core mission would be the creation of original knowledge and
   538→understanding and in which research would be pre-eminent. These advocates
   539→included Friedrich Schliermacher, Johann Fichte, and Wilhelm von Humboldt
   540→(see Watson 2010).
   541→It was von Humboldt who was able to implement this new model when, in
   542→1809, he became Under-Secretary in the Ministry of the Interior and responsible
   543→for education in Prussia. Although he was in ofﬁce for a mere 16 months, he
   544→initiated reforms designed, as Nybom (2003: 152) has put it, ‘to create an entirely
   545→new type of academic institutional order and a new type of academic’.
   546→With regard to the former, he took the lead in reorganising the University of
   547→Berlin which in 1810 became the world’s ﬁrst explicitly research-led university.
   548→With regard to the latter, the new type of academic was to be engaged both in
   549→DOI: 10.4324/9781003432159-3
   550→
   551→8
   552→
   553→The context
   554→
   555→extending knowledge and understanding (research) and transmitting it (educa­
   556→tion/teaching). Under the new university’s statutes training for this role was pro­
   557→vided by completing a new kind of doctorate to be awarded to a researcher who
   558→had made a signiﬁcant and original contribution to knowledge and understanding
   559→in the sciences (see Clark 2006). To signify its academic as opposed to professional
   560→status, this doctorate was awarded in ‘Die mutter aller Wissenschaften’ (the
   561→mother of all sciences), namely philosophy.
   562→The DPhil., or as it subsequently became, the PhD, was intended for a handful
   563→of the brightest and the best who would become the next generation of aca­
   564→demics; it was based on a ‘master-apprentice’ model of delivery; supervision was,
   565→as Park (2005: 15) has put it, viewed as a ‘secret garden or an activity that takes
   566→place behind closed doors between consenting adults’; candidates had to complete
   567→a research project, write it up in the form of a thesis, and defend it at an oral
   568→examination; and because it involved the creation of new knowledge, completion
   569→took as long as it took (Archbald 2011).
   570→What subsequently became known as the Humboldtian model of the doctorate
   571→(see Ash 2006) was copied during the 19th century in a few other countries,
   572→including France and the Netherlands (see Bogle 2017) and Russia (see Smo­
   573→lentseva 2007). But if elsewhere the universities of the Old World paid little atten­
   574→tion to the new degree, it was embraced by those of the New World, the United
   575→States. There, the ﬁrst doctorate was awarded by Yale in 1861, and the precedent
   576→was quickly followed by Harvard, Michigan, and Pennsylvania. The Humboldtian
   577→model was also copied in Japan, where the ﬁrst doctoral programme was established
   578→in 1887 at the founding of what is now the University of Tokyo (Yamamoto 2020).
   579→The next extension of the doctorate was during the First World War when it
   580→became apparent that Germany’s war effort had beneﬁtted signiﬁcantly from the
   581→strength of the country’s scientiﬁc base. For this reason, as well as concerns about
   582→candidates seeking doctoral studies elsewhere, the wartime coalition in the UK
   583→pressed universities to introduce the degree. The ﬁrst was awarded by Oxford in
   584→1920 and by 1921 the doctorate had been adopted by all of the UK universities.
   585→(Simpson 1983). In the mid-1920s it was introduced in Ireland (Share 2016) and
   586→South Africa (Herman 2015). At the end of that decade, Mexico became the ﬁrst
   587→Latin American country to adopt the degree (see Malo 2007) and in the 1930s
   588→the ﬁrst doctorates were awarded in Brazil (see Ribeiro 2007). In New Zealand,
   589→following the initial implementation of the doctorate in the 1920s, it was perma­
   590→nently reinstated in 1944. After the Second World War, Australia adopted the new
   591→degree and the ﬁrst doctorate was awarded in 1948 (Dobson 2012).
   592→By the late 1940s, the PhD had spread throughout the Anglo-American world,
   593→but it was still resisted in universities across much of Western Europe, which clung
   594→on to their historical professional licentiates. However, the 1950s and 1960s saw
   595→research rise to the top of the political agenda as a key to both economic growth
   596→and defence capability, and the need to train researchers was reﬂected in a rapid
   597→growth of PhD programmes in Western European countries, with the ﬁnal adop­
   598→ters being Italy (1988) and Denmark (1989).

   604→By the late 1980s, the PhD had conquered Western Europe, but not Eastern
   605→Europe. In most of what was then the ‘Soviet bloc’, there were different arrange­
   606→ments based on the USSR model of a two-step doctorate which could be taken inside
   607→or outside the universities, for example in the Academies of Sciences, and in either
   608→case was subject to state approval (see Connelly 2000). However, the withdrawal of
   609→the Soviet Union after the ‘velvet revolutions’ at the end of the 1980s and start of the
   610→1990s was followed in many, although not all, of the newly-liberated countries by a
   611→re-organisation of graduate education and the establishment or re-establishment of
   612→the PhD in the universities (see Smolentseva, Huisman, and Froumin 2018, Yekibay
   613→et al. 2022).
   614→Further adopters of the doctorate in the third quarter of the 20th century were
   615→in Asia. In China, the ﬁrst formal programme began in 1978 with 12 doctoral
   616→candidates; within 20 years there were over 50,000 (see Bao, Kehm, and Ma
   617→2016, Huang 2017). In the 1980s and 1990s doctoral programmes were also
   618→established or expanded in many other Asian countries, including Bangladesh,
   619→India, Malaysia, Pakistan, Thailand, and Singapore (UNESCO 2014, Zhaung,
   620→Liu, and Ding 2022).
   621→Therefore, by the 1990s, the PhD had become a qualiﬁcation delivered over a
   622→large proportion of the globe. In its essentials, it was recognisable as the degree which
   623→had been introduced at the University of Berlin just under two centuries earlier.

   625→Developments in the doctorate in the late 20th and 21st centuries
   626→However, over the last three decades or so, as Hammond et al. (2010) have sug­
   627→gested, the doctorate has been transformed by four major clusters of develop­
   628→ments, namely formalisation, the growth and diversiﬁcation of the candidate
   629→population, the diversiﬁcation of modes of study, and the diversiﬁcation of pur­
   630→poses. Each of these can be disaggregated into a number of sub-headings.
   631→Formalisation
   632→Historically, doctoral education was largely left to supervisors and candidates with­
   633→out external intervention. But it has now been formalised through the processes of
   634→commodiﬁcation, McDonaldisation, regulation, collectivisation, and structuration.
   635→Commodiﬁcation
   636→As Taylor (2012: 122) notes: ‘The traditional model of the relationship between
   637→students and supervisors was that of master and apprentice whereby the latter
   638→learned by emulating the former.’ But, in the same way as undergraduate and
   639→taught postgraduate education has been fashioned by neo-liberal government
   640→policies into a ‘provider-consumer’ framework, so has doctoral education with
   641→candidates being encouraged to expect their supervisors to offer a service in
   642→supporting their research projects (see for example Denicolo, Duke, and
   643→
   644→10
   645→
   646→The context
   647→
   648→Reeves 2020, Bastalich and McCulloch 2022, Hillebrand and Leysinger 2023).
   649→If the project goes wrong or falls behind schedule, candidates are much more
   650→likely to look towards their supervisor for assistance and, if this is deemed to
   651→be inadequate, to take matters further. Supervisors then need to pay particular
   652→attention to developing and sustaining productive relationships with candidates
   653→as outlined in Chapter 6 and the development of timelines and milestones set
   654→out in Chapter 11.
   655→McDonaldisation
   656→Historically, neither institutions nor research sponsors took much interest in whe­
   657→ther doctoral candidates completed their degrees and, if so, how long it was taking
   658→them. But during the third quarter of the 20th century, governments began
   659→enquiring more closely about what they were getting in return for funding doc­
   660→toral research, and the answers, at least in their view, were unsatisfactory. Studies
   661→of completion rates in Western Europe (see Sadak 2004), the US (Golde 2005),
   662→Canada (Elgar and Klein 2004), and Australia (Bourke, Hattie, and Anderson
   663→2004) all suggested that around one half of doctoral students failed to gain their
   664→degrees and, of those who did, relatively few completed within the allotted time.
   665→In response, many governments and research sponsors resorted to what amoun­
   666→ted, in one form or another, to compulsion.
   667→Such compulsion forced institutions to act to improve submission and qualiﬁ­
   668→cation rates, which in turn led to pressures on supervisors to ensure that candidates
   669→submit and qualify within the allotted time span (see Soin and Huber 2021).
   670→Issues and strategies which supervisors might adopt such as the introduction of
   671→explicit milestones relating to progress are outlined in Chapter 11.
   672→Regulation
   673→Historically, neither research sponsors nor institutions enquired too closely about
   674→what went on in doctoral education generally or with regard to supervision in
   675→particular. However, in recent years concerns about the quality of the student
   676→experience and completion rates and times have led to the widespread introduc­
   677→tion of regulation. A number of countries now have national codes of practice
   678→which are replicated at institutional level or institutions have developed their own
   679→codes (see Bitusikova et al. 2010, Byrne, Jorgensen, and Loukkola 2013, Andres
   680→et al. 2015, Nerad 2020).
   681→So supervision has become a heavily regulated area of academic practice (see
   682→Guccione 2018, Hasgall, Saenan, and Borrell-Damian 2019), and it has become
   683→important that supervisors are aware of all of the relevant institutional rules and
   684→regulations, as discussed in Chapters 2, 5, 6, 8 11, and 19.
   685→A further development with regard to regulation has been in relation to the con­
   686→duct of supervisors with their candidates. Supervisory relationships are by their very
   687→nature unequal, with the supervisor being in a position of power over candidates,
   688→

   694→their position and behaved in ways that are unacceptable, including bullying (see for
   695→example English, Flaherty, and English 2018), discrimination (see for example
   696→Spencer 2021), and sexual harassment (see for example Bloom et al. 2021).
   697→While for many years such misconduct was brushed under the carpet, in recent
   698→years it has been exposed and in some countries national bodies and/or institu­
   699→tions have issued codes of conduct for supervisors, of which the latter need to be
   700→aware. These matters are discussed in Chapters 6, 15 and 16.
   701→Collectivisation
   702→The historical model of supervision has been one of research students having a
   703→single supervisor. However, in recent years in many countries there have been
   704→moves to collectivise supervision by establishing supervisory teams (see Kalman
   705→et al. 2022, Wald, Kumar, and Sanderson 2022). While this can yield advan­
   706→tages, it can also cause problems in so far as the relationship between super­
   707→visors and students becomes more diffused and potentially subject to conﬂict
   708→over competing intellectual perspectives, interpretations of projects, personal­
   709→ities, supervisory roles, and supervisory styles. These issues are discussed further
   710→in Chapter 7.
   711→Structuration
   712→Originally, doctoral education took the form of individual study; the supervisor
   713→provided guidance, the candidate worked independently on his or her own.
   714→While that largely remains the case in some countries even today, in many others
   715→the desire to provide better training for researchers, improve submission and
   716→completion times, and enhance employability has led institutions to establish
   717→appropriate structures for doctoral studies. These include various graduate
   718→schools, doctoral colleges, doctoral research schools, and doctoral training cen­
   719→tres and partnerships (see Hasgall, Saenan, and Borrell-Damian 2019, Kovacevic,
   720→Bitusikova, and Dagen2022).
   721→They have also led to a move toward more structured programmes of study. The
   722→latter normally include a curriculum in the forms of a taught research training
   723→component, rigorous monitoring procedures, and a generic skills component relat­
   724→ing to employability (see Kiley 2014, Gonzalez-Ocampo et al. 2015, Elliot et al.
   725→2020, European Universities Association/Council for Doctoral Education 2022).
   726→In these ways, doctoral education has increasingly become structured at both
   727→institutional and the programme levels (see Bitusikova 2010, Kiley 2011, Andres
   728→et al. 2015, McGloin and Wynne 2015, Grant et al. 2022, McKenna and van
   729→Schalwyk 2023). Again, this means that supervisors need not only to understand
   730→the disciplinary context of doctoral studies (Chapter 3) but the institutional ones
   731→(Chapter 2) and programme ones (Chapter 4) as well.

   737→Growth and diversiﬁcation of the candidate population
   738→Massiﬁcation
   739→Up to a few years ago, doctoral candidates were relatively few in number. But over
   740→the past two decades the numbers registering for doctoral programmes has grown
   741→rapidly across most of the globe (Pederson 2014, Andres et al. 2015, Bao, Kehm, and
   742→Ma 2016, Huang 2017, Shin, Kehm, and Jones 2018, Sarrico 2022). However, the
   743→number of supervisors has not increased pro rata, with the result that supervisors have
   744→more candidates to supervise. In this context, it is important that they are aware of
   745→institutional policies relating to supervisory workloads and limits on candidate num­
   746→bers (see Chapter 2) and of strategies for group supervision (see Chapter 6).
   747→Domestic diversiﬁcation
   748→Moreover, this expansion in numbers has been associated with an increase in the
   749→diversity of the domestic graduate population Traditionally the stereotype doctoral
   750→candidate was male, young, single, white, heterosexual, and without a disability.
   751→Now, in many countries a majority of candidates are female, signiﬁcant numbers are
   752→in their thirties or forties or older and have partners and families. While parity is a
   753→long way off, in many cases there has been an increasing representation of indigen­
   754→ous populations and ethnic and racial minority groups. Also, candidates have
   755→become more diverse in their sexuality, and more have reported disabilities. The
   756→needs of such candidates have come sharply into focus in recent years, and the
   757→implications for their supervisory practice are discussed further in Chapters 5 and 15.
   758→International diversiﬁcation
   759→A further trend has been the internationalisation of doctoral education in the sense of
   760→candidates studying for their doctorates in a country other than that of their origin.
   761→While there is nothing new in this in principle – for example in the 19th century many
   762→US and UK chemists did their doctoral studies in Germany (Simpson, 1983) – what has
   763→changed very rapidly in recent years is a large-scale movement to study abroad for doc­
   764→torates, particularly from the Southern to the Northern hemisphere. While this trend
   765→was halted or reversed during the Covid-19 pandemic, subsequently it seems to have
   766→resumed (de Wit and Altbach 2022) and signiﬁcant proportions of doctoral students are
   767→now from countries other than the one in which they are studying for their degrees.
   768→They bring huge beneﬁts to their host country, but may face challenges in their doctoral
   769→studies which supervisors can help them overcome, as outlined in Chapter 16.
   770→Obligation
   771→The traditional view was that, as doctoral candidates were adults, they should be
   772→responsible for their own wellbeing and mental health. However, in recent years a
   773→

   777→
   778→substantial volume of evidence (see for example Levecque et al. 2017, Marais et al.
   779→2017, Mackie and Bates 2018, Metcalfe, Wilson, and Levecque 2018) has accrued
   780→which suggests that doctoral candidates are signiﬁcantly more likely than compar­
   781→able groups of the population to suffer from low levels of wellbeing, mental dis­
   782→tress (usually depression and anxiety), and mental illness (clinically-proven
   783→depression and anxiety as well as more severe illnesses such as bi-polar and psy­
   784→chosis). In view of these ﬁndings, institutions have had to accept that they have a
   785→duty of care for the wellbeing and mental health of doctoral candidates. In part,
   786→this has devolved upon supervisors as they are in personal contact with students
   787→and hence most likely to be able to notice distress and direct candidates to sources
   788→of support, as discussed in Chapter 12.
   789→Diversiﬁcation of modes of study
   790→Casualisation
   791→As Taylor (2012: 121) has noted, ‘Historically, the model has been for doctoral
   792→students to be full-time. While this largely remains the case in the natural sciences,
   793→the picture can be very different in the arts, humanities, and social sciences where
   794→part-time study is becoming much more common’. As a result, part-timers have
   795→come to comprise a signiﬁcant proportion of the candidate population in a
   796→number of countries. They are often older with family and employment commit­
   797→ments which can affect their studies and of which supervisors need to be aware.
   798→Issues in relation to supervising part-time candidates are discussed in Chapter 17.
   799→Dislocation
   800→Traditionally, doctoral candidates studied on campus, but the advances in infor­
   801→mation and communications technology have meant that they can study at a
   802→considerable distance from the host institution within a country, or even in
   803→another country. In such cases, face-to-face contact between supervisors and stu­
   804→dents may be infrequent and replaced by telephonic and electronic communica­
   805→tion. The use of such media to supervise doctoral candidates was growing before
   806→the Covid pandemic, but during the latter online supervision became the norm, as
   807→did online examination. These developments have left legacies for supervisors
   808→which are explored in Chapters 17 and 19.
   809→Diversiﬁcation of purposes of study
   810→Cross-fertilisation
   811→Traditionally, doctorates have been undertaken within single disciplines. However,
   812→tackling many of the major problems and issues in contemporary research often
   813→involves the cross-fertilisation of insights from two or more disciplines (see Rashid
   814→

   820→disciplinary. Where supervisors themselves have a mono-disciplinary pedigree,
   821→operating across two or more can be challenging and the implications of this for
   822→supervision are discussed in Chapter 3.
   823→Proliferation
   824→Whereas the PhD has been the dominant research degree for the past two cen­
   825→turies, in the last two decades other forms of doctoral degrees have been devel­
   826→oped, including doctorates by publication, practice-led doctorates, professional
   827→doctorates, collaborative doctorates, and hybrids involving two or more of these
   828→variants. The implications of these developments for supervision are considered in
   829→Chapters 4 and 7.
   830→Capitalisation
   831→Historically again, the primary purpose of the doctorate has been to reproduce the
   832→academic workforce, that is, to train new generations of researchers for the uni­
   833→versities. However, this has changed, partly reﬂecting a paucity of tenured aca­
   834→demic posts in some countries, the attractiveness of employment opportunities
   835→outside academia, and a re-branding of the purpose of the doctorate to supply
   836→human capital for the knowledge economies.
   837→For these reasons, there is now a presumption that doctoral programmes will
   838→include the development of the relevant generic skills to support doctoral gradu­
   839→ates to gain employment inside or outside academia, So as well as their traditional
   840→role in supporting candidates to becoming academics, supervisors may be expected
   841→to support the development of the generic skills needed for them to enter a wider
   842→range of employments.
   843→The roles of supervisors in supporting candidates to prepare for academic and
   844→non-academic roles are discussed in Chapter 14.

   846→Conclusions
   847→The effect of the changes outlined has been to transform doctoral education vir­
   848→tually across the globe. What was the last ‘cottage industry’ in higher education
   849→has become commodiﬁed, McDonaldised, collectivised, regulated, structured, and
   850→increasingly collaborative; from being mono-disciplinary, many doctoral research
   851→projects are interdisciplinary: as well as the traditional PhD, there are numerous
   852→other types of doctorates; the precocious few candidates have become the diverse
   853→many; obligations have come to the fore with regard to supervisors’ conduct
   854→towards candidates and their responsibilities for their welfare; increasingly instead
   855→of only studying on campus, candidates may be studying at a distance and being
   856→examined online; doctoral studies have become increasingly directed towards
   857→projects which are seen to have potential for a wider impact than academia; and,
   858→
   859→The overall context
   860→
   861→15
   862→
   863→rather than becoming academics, doctoral graduates are increasingly engaged in
   864→other forms of employment.
   865→These changes have profoundly affected doctoral supervision, and there is some
   866→evidence that this has been recognised by supervisors themselves. So, for example,
   867→when Halse and Malfoy (2010: 80) conducted a survey of experienced doctoral
   868→supervisors, they found that all of their respondents:
   869→testiﬁed to the changes in their work. They described doctoral supervision
   870→between the 1970s and the 1990s as an intellectual and social enterprise,
   871→where personal boundaries were sometimes blurred but the roles of super­
   872→visors and students were clear: the supervisor provided oversight and gui­
   873→dance; the doctoral student was responsible for producing a seminal thesis that
   874→would secure his or her place as an authority within the ﬁeld … all super­
   875→visors … conceptualised their current work with doctoral students as sig­
   876→niﬁcantly different from their former experiences. They spoke of actively
   877→fashioning students’ development to address deﬁcits in expertise; deliberately
   878→intervening to ensure timely completion; purposeful provision of tutoring or
   879→research assistance to minimise the risk of ﬁnancial distress and withdrawal;
   880→and the deployment of personal networks to facilitate completion and ensure
   881→post-graduation employment.
   882→The remainder of this book then seeks to assist supervisors in engaging with these
   883→challenges and, where appropriate, to consider how to enhance their practice.

  1094→Introduction
  1095→The developments in doctoral education described in the previous chapter are
  1096→often only apparent to supervisors in their translation into the broad setting in
  1097→which they work, namely their institutions. It is the latter, of course, that ulti­
  1098→mately award doctoral degrees and are responsible for the standards of awards, for
  1099→setting expectations of supervisors, for deciding who is eligible to supervise, and
  1100→for providing the support necessary for them to supervise effectively.
  1101→Institutions can and do vary in all of these matters, and for this reason it is
  1102→important that supervisors do not simply rely upon their own experience to inform
  1103→their practice but make efforts to understand the institutional context within
  1104→which they are supervising.
  1105→
  1106→The standards of doctoral awards
  1107→The ultimate object of the exercise is for the candidate to gain a doctorate, which
  1108→involves them meeting the standards set for the award. In order to supervise can­
  1109→didates to achieve those standards, supervisors obviously need to know what the
  1110→standards are. If they do not, then it effectively means that both are operating in
  1111→the dark, and this can be a recipe for disaster.
  1112→Of course, nearly all supervisors will have a doctorate themselves, and on this
  1113→basis they will have some understanding of the standards for the award. But,
  1114→bearing in mind that this is only a sample of one and may have been gained in a
  1115→different institution or a different doctoral programme or in a different higher
  1116→education system, they need to consult the formal deﬁnition.
  1117→In some countries with relatively centralised higher education systems, the
  1118→standards of doctorates are legally deﬁned by the state, as for example in France
  1119→(Batteau 2021) and Japan (Yamamoto 2021), but in others they are deﬁned by
  1120→institutions, and are usually set out in their regulations or ordinances.
  1121→Historically, these have not necessarily been the most enlightening sources of
  1122→information, but in recent years, institutions have been required by external

  1367→Eligibility to supervise
  1368→In a few countries, eligibility to supervise is deﬁned by the state. So, for example,
  1369→in China academic staff must normally be full professors and have been pro­
  1370→moted to a higher grade in order to supervise (Lin and Liu, 2021). In France
  1371→(see Batteau 2021) and Poland (Kweik 2021) supervisors must by law have a
  1372→higher doctorate, the ‘habilitation’. In Sweden, it has been mandatory since
  1373→2004 for at least one supervisor to have attended the relevant institutional
  1374→training programme (Emilsson and Johnsson 2007).
  1375→In most countries, however, it is ultimately up to institutions to deﬁne who is
  1376→eligible to supervise doctoral students. Usually, it is a requirement that supervisors
  1377→themselves have a doctorate, although in some circumstances – particularly in
  1378→disciplines where the doctorate has been a relatively recent innovation – the
  1379→requirement may be for them to have equivalent research experience. A further
  1380→requirement in most cases that they are research active (see Taylor, Kiley, and
  1381→Holley 2021).
  1382→In many institutions, as well as the above, as will be seen in the next section,
  1383→there may be a further requirement that new supervisors undertake appropriate
  1384→initial professional development in the form of a course or training programme.
  1385→Additionally, some institutions may require supervisors to initially act as a
  1386→second/associate or junior supervisor in a team rather than as a primary supervisor.
  1387→Here, the normal requirement is to complete at least one supervisory cycle as a
  1388→second/associate supervisor under the wing of a more experienced senior super­
  1389→visor before becoming eligible to be a primary supervisor.
  1390→Finally, institutions may vary the number of candidates who can be supervised
  1391→on the basis of experience of successful completions; the more of the latter, the
  1392→more candidates can be supervised.

  1397→Registered status – this allows staff members or adjunct appointments

  1399→up to three students. To qualify, they need to have a doctorate or equivalent
  1400→research experience, and have completed required training.
  1401→Accredited status – this allows staff members or adjunct appointments to be
  1402→a main supervisor for up to eight candidates and an associate supervisor for a
  1403→further eight with a 10% to 90% supervisory load per student. To qualify, they
  1404→need to have a doctorate, at least one year’s full-time equivalent experience in
  1405→supervising a doctoral student or students, meet Faculty-speciﬁc quality and
  1406→research active criteria, and to have completed required training.
  1407→Accredited Emeritus status – this allows staff allows staff approaching
  1408→retirement to complete any current supervisions but not take on new

  1436→Support for supervisors
  1437→Support for supervisors may include initial professional development at the start of
  1438→their supervisory careers, opportunities for being mentored while acting as a
  1439→second supervisor, and opportunities for continuing professional development.
  1440→Additionally, institutions may also provide support in the event of difﬁculties in the
  1441→relationship with candidates, of which supervisors also need to be aware.
  1442→Initial professional development
  1443→Left to their own devices new supervisors tend to rely heavily upon their own
  1444→experiences as doctoral candidates to inform their practice (see for example
  1445→Amundsen and McAlpine 2009; Guerin, Kerr, and Green 2015, Fulgence, 2019,
  1446→Gonzalez-Ocampo and Castello 2019, Whittington, Barnes, and Lee 2021).
  1447→Where these experiences are good, they tend to emulate whatever their own
  1448→supervisors did; where they are poor, often they do the exact opposite. But neither
  1449→emulation nor rejection based on personal experience is likely to offer a sound
  1450→pedagogical basis upon which to be a successful supervisor, and there is evidence
  1451→(see for example Lovitts 2008, Wadesango and Machingambi 2011, Van de
  1452→

  1454→

  1456→
  1457→Schoot et al. 2013, Litalien and Guay 2015) that the quality of supervision is a
  1458→major factor in explaining delayed and non-completion of candidature.
  1459→Primarily for this reason many, if by no means all, institutions have estab­
  1460→lished mandatory initial professional development programmes for academic
  1461→staff who are new to supervision. This is almost universal in Australia (see Kiley
  1462→2021), Ireland (see Carton 2021), Sweden (see Brodin, Sonesson, and Ahlberg
  1463→2021), and the UK (Taylor 2018) but much patchier in other countries, for
  1464→example France (see Batteau 2021) or Germany (see Brentel 2021). In other
  1465→cases, there may be voluntary opportunities for professional development in
  1466→supervisory practice.
  1467→As Kiley (2021), Guerin et al. (2017) and Taylor (2018) have shown, there are
  1468→considerable variations in the duration and substance of initial professional devel­
  1469→opment programmes. The most basic generally lasts two or three hours and has a
  1470→focus on what might be described as compliance. In these sessions staff new to
  1471→research supervision are taken through the main rules and procedures relating to
  1472→being a supervisor at the particular institution, but not otherwise equipped for
  1473→their roles.
  1474→
  1475→Example
  1476→Feather and McDermott (2014: 169) commented on a supervisor training
  1477→course they had been required to attend and reported that:
  1478→although [it] was insightful and engaging, we did not learn much more
  1479→than we already knew. The programme dealt mainly with the mechanics
  1480→(form-ﬁlling, administration, recording of attendance, and other
  1481→administrative tasks) of supervising PhDs, but not the realities of
  1482→supervision: that is, the emotions, the mentoring of students (both
  1483→domestic and international) and the softer issues of dealing with stu­
  1484→dents and their needs. So this left both of us with anxieties and ques­
  1485→tions concerning the supervision of doctoral students; more so as every
  1486→member of staff was expected to take on more doctoral students by the
  1487→school and the university.
  1488→
  1489→Perhaps a more common format is the one-day introductory workshop/online
  1490→modules which, when completed, allow a staff member to take on a supervisory
  1491→role, although generally as a second/associate supervisor with a more experienced
  1492→colleague managing or chairing the overall supervisory team. Such programmes
  1493→may contain pedagogical elements, but often leave out other aspects of supervisory
  1494→responsibilities (see Taylor 2018).
  1495→In other cases, initial professional development programmes are much more
  1496→comprehensive.

  1751→Conclusions
  1752→Hopefully, the present chapter has indicated the importance of supervisors being
  1753→aware of the institutional framework within which they are operating in terms of
  1754→standards, expectations, eligibility, and support. Often, these matters are covered
  1755→in induction or initial professional development workshops for new supervisors
  1756→who are thereby made aware of them at the time that they begin their supervisory
  1757→careers. But is worth noting that, as doctoral education itself is changing and is
  1758→impacted by events such as the Covid-19 pandemic, institutions are having to
  1759→follow suit, with the result that frameworks are constantly being modiﬁed and
  1760→updated. So deﬁnitions of the doctorate are being modiﬁed by the appearance of
  1761→new supra-national and national frameworks; expectations are constantly expand­
  1762→ing, for example through the new agendas of timely submission and completion,
  1763→the quality of the candidate experience, and employability; eligibility is becoming
  1764→more rigorous through the professionalisation of supervision; and support is
  1765→becoming more extensive, particularly in terms of professional development.
  1766→The point here is that it is not enough for supervisors to be aware of the insti­
  1767→tutional framework at whatever time they were taken through it in the past; they
  1768→need to keep up to date on current developments and, where appropriate, embody
  1769→them in their practice.
