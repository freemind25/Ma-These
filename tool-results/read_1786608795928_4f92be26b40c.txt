   664→The gratifying response to the previous editions of this book testifies to the
   665→need of research students and their supervisors to understand the processes
   666→of effective doctoral education. The number of translations into other
   667→languages – Reformed Chinese, Spanish, Portuguese, Classical Chinese,
   668→Russian, Arabic and Korean (in chronological order) – demonstrates that
   669→the issues covered here are highly relevant in many countries. This need to
   670→understand is reinforced by the considerable institutional change taking
   671→place in the higher education system in the UK. Since our first edition in
   672→1987 opened up the subject for debate, many of the developments we have
   673→advocated have come about: greater university recognition and support
   674→for doctoral students, effective monitoring of student progress, training
   675→for supervisors in teaching the craft of research and so on. And the changes
   676→are continuing apace. It is therefore appropriate to offer a fourth edition,
   677→revised and updated to the present situation.
   678→One comment made in the generally favourable reviews of the first edition deserves attention here. In our analysis of the complex tasks of PhD
   679→study, we describe the difficulties which may be encountered. This is in
   680→order to enable both students and supervisors to avoid such problems or to
   681→overcome them. It has been suggested that this inevitably gives too great a
   682→focus on the ‘pathologies’ of the doctoral process. We fear that this may be
   683→true, and so we should like to reiterate here the positive aspects of being a
   684→PhD student. The joys of doing research are considerable, and anyone in a
   685→position to carry out research is indeed privileged. Feelings of exploration,
   686→excitement, challenge, involvement and passion are frequent and are
   687→commented on in this book. The enormous feeling of achievement on the
   688→award of the degree lasts for many throughout their whole lives. Clearly

   735→STUDENT

   739→
   740→This book is a handbook and a survival manual for PhD students. If you are
   741→intending to embark on a research degree it will introduce you to the
   742→system and, by increasing your understanding, help you to improve your
   743→choice of university, college, department and supervisor.
   744→If you have just picked this book up and you are already a research
   745→student, then you should read it thoroughly – and hang on to it so that
   746→you can refer to it frequently. You will need to do this because we shall be
   747→discussing the skills and processes that are crucial to obtaining the PhD
   748→degree.
   749→If you are a supervisor, or contemplating becoming one, the book is
   750→highly relevant to you too, because it deals with the educational processes
   751→that it is your responsibility to encourage for the successful completion of
   752→your students’ research degrees.
   753→If you are a senior academic administrator, the relevance of this book is
   754→that it provides a guide to procedures and systems concerned with research
   755→degrees which will enable you to evaluate the adequacy of the provision
   756→your university is making for research students.
   757→The book focuses on process issues which are not discipline-specific. It
   758→cannot help you to design an investigation or an experiment as these
   759→activities require professional knowledge of your particular field. Similarly
   760→it does not deal with the financial difficulties of doctoral students, which
   761→will vary considerably depending on your circumstances. Nor does it consider factors impinging on you after you have completed your course such
   762→as the employment options available to PhDs. (Delamont and Atkinson
   763→2004 discuss developing a postdoctoral research career.)
   764→But the book does suggest that you ponder on some basic questions
   765→

   769→
   770→HOW TO GET A PhD
   771→
   772→before embarking on a course of study leading to the PhD degree. Do you
   773→want to spend three to four years of your life doing research on one topic?
   774→Will you be satisfied to live on a student grant for that time? Are you
   775→committed to a PhD or would a professional doctorate (e.g. EdD, EngD)
   776→suit you better? (The differences are discussed on pp. 196ff. of this book.)
   777→Are you able to tolerate regular periods of intellectual loneliness when
   778→only you are responsible for producing ‘creative thoughts’? It is vital that
   779→you give a firm ‘yes’ in answer to all those questions. You must make the
   780→decision to study and work for your doctorate based on the sure knowledge that it is the right thing for you. If what you really want is to write a
   781→bestseller, then conducting research for a thesis is not the optimum way to
   782→go about it. Perhaps you don’t really know what you want to do with the
   783→rest of your life and continuing in the university system seems a good way
   784→of putting off that decision. If this is so then you have chosen an extremely
   785→difficult way of solving your particular problem.

   787→I
   788→
   789→The nature of doctoral education
   790→
   791→Acquiring the skills and understanding the processes necessary for success
   792→cannot be done at a single reading. As a research student you need continually to use the ideas in this book to develop your own insight into your
   793→own situation. In this way your professional learning will develop as it
   794→should – under your own management.
   795→‘Under your own management’ is the key to the nature of doctoral education. In undergraduate education a great deal, in academic terms, is
   796→organized for the student. It may not have seemed like that to you at the
   797→time, because you were required to do a considerable amount of work, but,
   798→for example, syllabuses were laid down, textbooks were specified, practical
   799→sessions were designed, the examinations were organized to cover a set
   800→range of topics in questions of a known form, and so on. You could quite
   801→reasonably have complained if asked about an extraneous subject, ‘But no
   802→one told me that I was supposed to learn that topic (or methodology or
   803→theory or historical period).’ For the most part you were following an
   804→academic course set by your teachers.
   805→In doctoral education, you have to take responsibility for managing
   806→your learning and for getting yourself a PhD. Of course, there will be
   807→people around to help you: – your supervisor(s), other academics in your
   808→department, fellow students and so on. Some of them will even tell you
   809→what, in their opinion, you have to do to obtain the degree, but the
   810→responsibility for determining what is required, as well as for carrying it
   811→out, remains firmly with you. And if it turns out that you need a particular
   812→topic or theory for your work, then it is no excuse to say, ‘But nobody told
   813→me it was relevant.’ It is your responsibility.

   824→you what to do next or, worse, complaining that nobody is telling you

   826→deficiencies.
   827→The overall university framework for research students ensures that
   828→there is a basic similarity for all doctoral candidates as they progress
   829→through their studies. But there are also some notable differences between
   830→the research cultures of university disciplines, particularly between the
   831→culture of the laboratory-based sciences and that of the humanities and
   832→social sciences. To a considerable extent they stem from the large capital
   833→investment in equipment and materials required in scientific research.
   834→Supervisors in science have to take the lead in obtaining the physical
   835→resources and the research personnel required. A studentship may be allocated and a doctoral student recruited specifically to work on a designated
   836→line of research. In this situation the ‘apprenticeship’ aspect of being a
   837→doctoral student is emphasized. The student’s research topic will be clearly
   838→defined to fit in with the innovative thrust of the supervisor’s research
   839→programme, and this will set limits to the level of research creativity that
   840→can be shown. The student will be required to do ‘dogsbody’ work in the
   841→laboratory or on the computer as part of professional training. In these
   842→situations there develops what might be called a ‘joint ownership’ of the
   843→doctoral research between supervisors and the students. Supervisors will
   844→have a strong interest in getting the research work done and using the
   845→results obtained. Joint papers will be the norm. The danger to watch for in
   846→this culture is the exploitation of the student, leading to the feeling of
   847→being just an extra pair of hands for the supervisors’ research. It must be
   848→remembered that there has to be a sufficient amount of autonomy for
   849→the student to be able to make an original contribution. It is this which
   850→justifies the award of the PhD degree.
   851→In contrast, in the humanities and the social sciences students often
   852→come with their own topics within the field in which the supervisor is
   853→expert, and academics give a service of research supervision. Being busy
   854→people, supervisors often have to ration the amount of attention they can
   855→give. Research supervision has to compete with the supervisor’s own current research (which can be considerably different), undergraduate teaching and administration. Supervisors will have only a general interest in the
   856→results of the student’s research, and will act more as role models than as
   857→apprentice-masters. The danger to watch for in this culture is the neglect
   858→of the student for long periods of weeks, months, even years. It must be
   859→remembered that students need the regular support of supervisors if they
   860→are to develop sufficiently to achieve the PhD degree.

   883→The psychology of being a research student
   884→
   885→New research students enter the system determined to make an outstanding contribution to their subject. By the time they start the final stages of
   886→thesis-writing for the degree they are determined to ‘get it and forget it!’
   887→During the intervening years their enthusiasm has been dampened by the
   888→demands of having to concentrate on a specific topic and conduct routine
   889→and repetitive tasks in an atmosphere where nobody seems either to
   890→understand or to care about their work.
   891→They come into the university or college knowing precisely who they
   892→are: successful and intelligent holders of well-earned qualifications. It is
   893→not long before they lose their initial confidence and begin to question
   894→their own self-image. This is the result of contacts (no matter how sporadic
   895→or from what distance) with academic discourse. Such contacts could
   896→come from members of staff, postgraduates who are further into their
   897→research than the first-year students, and papers published in journals or
   898→presented at conferences. These challenge the assumptions and conceptions that the young graduates had accepted as inviolable. From this
   899→period of self-doubt and questioning, the successful postgraduates emerge
   900→with a new identity as competent professionals, able to argue their viewpoint with anybody regardless of status, confident of their own knowledge
   901→but also aware of its boundaries. This new identity permits them to ask for
   902→information when they are aware that they don’t know something and to
   903→express a lack of understanding when this is necessary, instead of pretending that there is no difficulty for fear of being thought stupid. To arrive
   904→at this point is what being a postgraduate research student is really all
   905→about.

  1589→The meaning of a doctorate
  1590→
  1591→We are going to start with some historical background and present in a
  1592→schematic way the meaning of the degree structure of a British university.
  1593→ⵧ A bachelor’s degree traditionally meant that the recipient had obtained
  1594→a general education (specializing at this level is a relatively recent
  1595→nineteenth-century development).
  1596→ⵧ A master’s degree is a licence to practise. Originally this meant to
  1597→practise theology, that is, to take a living in the Church, but now there
  1598→are master’s degrees across a whole range of disciplines: business
  1599→administration, soil biology, computing, applied linguistics and so on.
  1600→The degree marks the possession of advanced knowledge in a specialist
  1601→field.

  1603→in a university as a member of a faculty. Nowadays this does not mean
  1604→that becoming a lecturer is the only reason for taking a doctorate, since
  1605→the degree has much wider career connotations outside academia and
  1606→many of those with doctorates do not have academic teaching posts.
  1607→The concept stems, though, from the need for a faculty member to be

  1614→

  1616→of current knowledge, and able to extend them. As the highest degree
  1617→that can be awarded, it proclaims that the recipient is worthy of being
  1618→listened to as an equal by the appropriate university faculty.

  1621→(Law), DMus (Music), DSc (Science), DLitt (Letters, i.e. Arts). These socalled ‘higher doctorates’ are awarded as a recognition of a substantial

  1662→university. In modern terms it is useful to think of this as becoming a fully
  1663→professional researcher in your field. Let us try to spell out what becoming
  1664→a full professional means:
  1665→1 At the most basic level it means that you have something to say that
  1666→your peers want to listen to.
  1667→2 In order to do this you must have a command of what is happening in
  1668→your subject so that you can evaluate the worth of what others are
  1669→doing.
  1670→3 You must have the astuteness to discover where you can make a useful
  1671→contribution.
  1672→4 You must be aware of the ethics of your profession and work within
  1673→them.
  1674→5 You must have mastery of appropriate techniques that are currently
  1675→being used, and also be aware of their limitations.
  1676→6 You must be able to communicate your results effectively in the
  1677→professional arena.
  1678→7 All this must be carried out in an international context; your professional peer group is worldwide. (It always was, of course, but the rate of
  1679→diffusion is infinitely faster than it used to be and with the World Wide

  1681→argued about, written and published by your academic community
  1682→across the world.
  1683→This list clearly represents quite a tall order, not least because, as you will
  1684→have spotted, most of the list concerns the learning of skills, not knowledge. The crucial distinction is between ‘knowing that’ and ‘knowing
  1685→how’, as the philosopher Gilbert Ryle put it. It is not enough for someone
  1686→to tell you that this is a fruitful area for study, that this technique is available for use, that you should write a clear paper communicating your
  1687→contribution. You have to be able to carve out a researchable topic, to
  1688→master the techniques required and put them to appropriate use, and to
  1689→cogently communicate your findings.
  1690→So there are craft skills involved in becoming a full professional, which,
  1691→like any skills, have to be learned by doing the task in practice situations
  1692→under supervision. The skills required cannot easily be stated by other
  1693→professionals, though many aspects can be learned from them – some
  1694→consciously, others unconsciously. But there have to be the twin elements
  1695→of exploration and practice, which are basic to all learning of skills. This is
  1696→why the PhD takes time.

  1954→Aims of examiners
  1955→
  1956→External examiners are academics from universities other than your own
  1957→and are used to ensure that, within a given discipline, standards of quality
  1958→for which the PhD degree is awarded are uniform across universities. Some
  1959→examiners see the aims of the PhD to be a training for a career in research,
  1960→some as an introduction to writing books, some as preparation for the
  1961→academic life and some, as we have suggested, to become a fully rounded
  1962→professional.
  1963→Whether examiners are more interested in the research, the thesis or the
  1964→performance of the candidate in the oral examination, they are looking for
  1965→a command of the subject area (or context) of the research, as well as the
  1966→specific topic. The British PhD is awarded for an original contribution to
  1967→knowledge. Yet, as we shall see in Chapter 6, originality in the PhD is a
  1968→complex concept which has not yet been adequately defined. Nevertheless, examiners need to be satisfied that the work has a degree of originality
  1969→and that it is the genuine work of the candidate.
  1970→Examiners acquire reputations for their performance in this role. Some
  1971→become known as difficult to please while others are prepared to take the
  1972→supervisor’s evaluation of the work almost without question. Some examiners make the oral examination a real test of professional knowledge and
  1973→exposition, while others allow it to be more of a relaxed conversation
  1974→between friends.
  1975→The reputations that the examiners acquire do sometimes affect their
  1976→selection, especially when it is left to the supervisor to choose. Some candidates find that their external examiners have been chosen on the basis of
  1977→how highly their supervisors regard the students’ work. For example, if a
  1978→supervisor thinks that a particular student will only just satisfy requirements, a less exacting examiner may be chosen. If, on the other hand, the
  1979→supervisor considers the student’s work to be of considerable merit a tough
  1980→examiner is chosen and the student then has the advantage of being
  1981→passed by somebody who adds prestige to the new PhD’s success. However,
  1982→such a system is far from universal and can be extremely unpopular.
