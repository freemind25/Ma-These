   564→THE SCOPE OF SCIENTIFIC WRITING
   565→The term scientific writing commonly denotes the reporting of original research
   566→in journals through scientific papers that follow a standard format. In its broader
   567→sense, scientific writing also includes communication about science through
   568→other types of journal articles, such as review papers summarizing and integrat-
   569→ing previously published research. And in a still broader sense, it includes other
   570→types of professional communication by scientists—for example, grant propos-
   571→als, oral presentations, and poster presentations. Related endeavors include
   572→writing about science for the public, sometimes called science writing.
   573→THE NEED FOR CLARITY
   574→The key characteristic of scientific writing is clarity. Successful scientific
   575→experimentation is the result of a clear mind attacking a clearly stated problem
   576→and producing clearly stated conclusions. Ideally, clarity should be a character-
   577→```
   578→istic of any type of communication; however, when something is being said for
   579→```
   580→the first time, clarity is essential. Most scientific papers, those published in our
   581→primary research journals, are accepted for publication precisely because they
   582→contribute new knowledge. Hence, we should demand absolute clarity in sci-
   583→entific writing.

   587→RECEIVING THE SIGNALS
   588→Many people have no doubt heard this question: If a tree falls in the forest and
   589→there is no one there to hear it fall, does it make a sound? The correct answer
   590→is no. Sound is more than pressure waves, and indeed there can be no sound
   591→without someone to hear it.
   592→Similarly, scientific communication is a two-way process. Just as a signal of
   593→```
   594→any kind is useless unless it is perceived, a published scientific paper (signal)
   595→```
   596→is useless unless it is both received and understood by its intended audience.
   597→Thus we can restate the axiom of science as follows: A scientific experiment is
   598→not complete until the results have been published and understood. A pub-
   599→lished paper is no more than pressure waves unless it is understood. Too
   600→many scientific papers fall silently in the woods.
   601→UNDERSTANDING THE SIGNALS
   602→Scientific writing is the transmission of a clear signal to a recipient. The words
   603→of the signal should be as clear, simple, and well ordered as possible. In
   604→scientific writing, there is little need for ornamentation. Flowery literary
   605→embellishments—metaphors, similes, idiomatic expressions—are very likely
   606→to cause confusion and should seldom be used in research papers.
   607→Science is simply too important to be communicated in anything other
   608→than words that have a certain meaning. And the meaning should be clear and

   833→THE IMRAD STORY
   834→The early journals published papers that we call descriptive. Typically, a scien-
   835→tist would report, “First, I saw this, and then I saw that,” or “First, I did this,
   836→and then I did that.” Often the observations were in simple chronological
   837→order.
   838→This descriptive style was appropriate for the kind of science then being
   839→reported. In fact, this straightforward style of reporting still is sometimes used
   840→in “letters” journals, case reports in medicine, geological surveys, and other
   841→publications.
   842→By the second half of the nineteenth century, science was beginning to move
   843→fast and in increasingly sophisticated ways. Microbiology serves as an example.
   844→Especially through the work of Louis Pasteur, who confirmed the germ theory
   845→of disease and developed pure-culture methods of studying microorganisms,
   846→both science and the reporting of science made great advances.
   847→At this time, methodology became all-important. To quiet his critics, many
   848→of whom were fanatic believers in the theory of spontaneous generation, Pas-
   849→teur found it necessary to describe his experiments in exquisite detail. Because
   850→reasonably competent peers could reproduce Pasteur’s experiments, the prin-
   851→ciple of reproducibility of experiments became a fundamental tenet of the phi-
   852→losophy of science, and a separate methods section led the way toward the
   853→highly structured IMRAD format.

  1217→Good writing tends to be largely a matter of good revising. No one will see

  1230→about 10 times.”

  1232→• Does the manuscript include all the information it should?

  1238→• Is everything logically organized?
  1239→• Is everything clearly worded?
  1240→• Have you stated your points briefly, simply, and directly? In other words,
  1241→is everything concise?

  1243→• Are all figures and tables well designed?

  1251→which focuses mainly on editing one’s own work.

  1253→and request their feedback. Years ago, scientists were advised, “Show your
  1254→manuscript to a guy in your lab, a guy in a lab down the hall, and your wife.”
  1255→These days, such advice would rightly be viewed as inaccurate and sexist. Yet
  1256→the concept remains valid. So, consider following this advice: Show your man-
  1257→uscript to an expert in your research specialty, who can help identify technical
  1258→problems. Also, show it to someone in your general field, who can note items

  1262→consider also showing your manuscript to a professional scientific editor, as
  1263→discussed in Chapter 41.

  1265→how to apply it. Of course, follow those suggestions that you find useful. Even
  1266→if a suggestion seems unsuitable, keep it in mind. Although you may disagree
