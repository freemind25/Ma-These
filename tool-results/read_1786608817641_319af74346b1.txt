   519→   519→Introduction
   520→   520→Supervisors tend to be immersed in their busy academic and/or professional lives,
   521→   521→not to mention their personal ones, and seldom have the time to maintain an
   522→   522→overview of ongoing developments and research in doctoral education. As a con­
   523→   523→sequence, many are experiencing new demands without necessarily understanding
   524→   524→the wider context within which they arise. The purpose of the present chapter is to
   525→   525→try to set that wider context. It is based on the work of Taylor (2012, 2023) and
   526→   526→begins with a brief summary of the historical origins and evolution of doctoral
   527→   527→education from its modern origins through to the 1980s and then looks at sub­
   528→   528→sequent developments that have transformed the ﬁeld and the context of
   529→   529→supervision.
   530→   530→
   531→   531→The origins and evolution of doctoral education
   532→   532→Doctorates have their origins in the mediaeval universities when they were used
   533→   533→primarily as a means of licensing teachers in the professions of medicine, law, and
   534→   534→theology (Bernstein et al. 2014, Amaral and Carvalho 2020). This paradigm
   535→   535→remained dominant until, in the early 19th century, a number of eminent
   536→   536→Enlightenment thinkers in Prussia began to advocate a new kind of university, one
   537→   537→in which the core mission would be the creation of original knowledge and
   538→   538→understanding and in which research would be pre-eminent. These advocates
   539→   539→included Friedrich Schliermacher, Johann Fichte, and Wilhelm von Humboldt
   540→   540→(see Watson 2010).
   541→   541→It was von Humboldt who was able to implement this new model when, in
   542→   542→1809, he became Under-Secretary in the Ministry of the Interior and responsible
   543→   543→for education in Prussia. Although he was in ofﬁce for a mere 16 months, he
   544→   544→initiated reforms designed, as Nybom (2003: 152) has put it, ‘to create an entirely
   545→   545→new type of academic institutional order and a new type of academic’.
   546→   546→With regard to the former, he took the lead in reorganising the University of
   547→   547→Berlin which in 1810 became the world’s ﬁrst explicitly research-led university.
   548→   548→With regard to the latter, the new type of academic was to be engaged both in

   555→   555→extending knowledge and understanding (research) and transmitting it (educa­
   556→   556→tion/teaching). Under the new university’s statutes training for this role was pro­
   557→   557→vided by completing a new kind of doctorate to be awarded to a researcher who
   558→   558→had made a signiﬁcant and original contribution to knowledge and understanding
   559→   559→in the sciences (see Clark 2006). To signify its academic as opposed to professional
   560→   560→status, this doctorate was awarded in ‘Die mutter aller Wissenschaften’ (the
   561→   561→mother of all sciences), namely philosophy.
   562→   562→The DPhil., or as it subsequently became, the PhD, was intended for a handful
   563→   563→of the brightest and the best who would become the next generation of aca­
   564→   564→demics; it was based on a ‘master-apprentice’ model of delivery; supervision was,
   565→   565→as Park (2005: 15) has put it, viewed as a ‘secret garden or an activity that takes
   566→   566→place behind closed doors between consenting adults’; candidates had to complete
   567→   567→a research project, write it up in the form of a thesis, and defend it at an oral
   568→   568→examination; and because it involved the creation of new knowledge, completion
   569→   569→took as long as it took (Archbald 2011).
   570→   570→What subsequently became known as the Humboldtian model of the doctorate
   571→   571→(see Ash 2006) was copied during the 19th century in a few other countries,
   572→   572→including France and the Netherlands (see Bogle 2017) and Russia (see Smo­
   573→   573→lentseva 2007). But if elsewhere the universities of the Old World paid little atten­
   574→   574→tion to the new degree, it was embraced by those of the New World, the United
   575→   575→States. There, the ﬁrst doctorate was awarded by Yale in 1861, and the precedent
   576→   576→was quickly followed by Harvard, Michigan, and Pennsylvania. The Humboldtian
   577→   577→model was also copied in Japan, where the ﬁrst doctoral programme was established
   578→   578→in 1887 at the founding of what is now the University of Tokyo (Yamamoto 2020).
   579→   579→The next extension of the doctorate was during the First World War when it
   580→   580→became apparent that Germany’s war effort had beneﬁtted signiﬁcantly from the
   581→   581→strength of the country’s scientiﬁc base. For this reason, as well as concerns about
   582→   582→candidates seeking doctoral studies elsewhere, the wartime coalition in the UK
   583→   583→pressed universities to introduce the degree. The ﬁrst was awarded by Oxford in
   584→   584→1920 and by 1921 the doctorate had been adopted by all of the UK universities.
   585→   585→(Simpson 1983). In the mid-1920s it was introduced in Ireland (Share 2016) and
   586→   586→South Africa (Herman 2015). At the end of that decade, Mexico became the ﬁrst
   587→   587→Latin American country to adopt the degree (see Malo 2007) and in the 1930s
   588→   588→the ﬁrst doctorates were awarded in Brazil (see Ribeiro 2007). In New Zealand,
   589→   589→following the initial implementation of the doctorate in the 1920s, it was perma­
   590→   590→nently reinstated in 1944. After the Second World War, Australia adopted the new
   591→   591→degree and the ﬁrst doctorate was awarded in 1948 (Dobson 2012).
   592→   592→By the late 1940s, the PhD had spread throughout the Anglo-American world,
   593→   593→but it was still resisted in universities across much of Western Europe, which clung
   594→   594→on to their historical professional licentiates. However, the 1950s and 1960s saw
   595→   595→research rise to the top of the political agenda as a key to both economic growth
   596→   596→and defence capability, and the need to train researchers was reﬂected in a rapid
   597→   597→growth of PhD programmes in Western European countries, with the ﬁnal adop­
   598→   598→ters being Italy (1988) and Denmark (1989).

   604→   604→By the late 1980s, the PhD had conquered Western Europe, but not Eastern
   605→   605→Europe. In most of what was then the ‘Soviet bloc’, there were different arrange­
   606→   606→ments based on the USSR model of a two-step doctorate which could be taken inside
   607→   607→or outside the universities, for example in the Academies of Sciences, and in either
   608→   608→case was subject to state approval (see Connelly 2000). However, the withdrawal of
   609→   609→the Soviet Union after the ‘velvet revolutions’ at the end of the 1980s and start of the
   610→   610→1990s was followed in many, although not all, of the newly-liberated countries by a
   611→   611→re-organisation of graduate education and the establishment or re-establishment of
   612→   612→the PhD in the universities (see Smolentseva, Huisman, and Froumin 2018, Yekibay
   613→   613→et al. 2022).
   614→   614→Further adopters of the doctorate in the third quarter of the 20th century were
   615→   615→in Asia. In China, the ﬁrst formal programme began in 1978 with 12 doctoral
   616→   616→candidates; within 20 years there were over 50,000 (see Bao, Kehm, and Ma
   617→   617→2016, Huang 2017). In the 1980s and 1990s doctoral programmes were also
   618→   618→established or expanded in many other Asian countries, including Bangladesh,
   619→   619→India, Malaysia, Pakistan, Thailand, and Singapore (UNESCO 2014, Zhaung,
   620→   620→Liu, and Ding 2022).
   621→   621→Therefore, by the 1990s, the PhD had become a qualiﬁcation delivered over a
   622→   622→large proportion of the globe. In its essentials, it was recognisable as the degree which
   623→   623→had been introduced at the University of Berlin just under two centuries earlier.
   624→   624→
   625→   625→Developments in the doctorate in the late 20th and 21st centuries
   626→   626→However, over the last three decades or so, as Hammond et al. (2010) have sug­
   627→   627→gested, the doctorate has been transformed by four major clusters of develop­
   628→   628→ments, namely formalisation, the growth and diversiﬁcation of the candidate
   629→   629→population, the diversiﬁcation of modes of study, and the diversiﬁcation of pur­
   630→   630→poses. Each of these can be disaggregated into a number of sub-headings.
   631→   631→Formalisation
   632→   632→Historically, doctoral education was largely left to supervisors and candidates with­
   633→   633→out external intervention. But it has now been formalised through the processes of
   634→   634→commodiﬁcation, McDonaldisation, regulation, collectivisation, and structuration.
   635→   635→Commodiﬁcation
   636→   636→As Taylor (2012: 122) notes: ‘The traditional model of the relationship between
   637→   637→students and supervisors was that of master and apprentice whereby the latter
   638→   638→learned by emulating the former.’ But, in the same way as undergraduate and
   639→   639→taught postgraduate education has been fashioned by neo-liberal government
   640→   640→policies into a ‘provider-consumer’ framework, so has doctoral education with
   641→   641→candidates being encouraged to expect their supervisors to offer a service in
   642→   642→supporting their research projects (see for example Denicolo, Duke, and
   643→   643→

   645→   645→
   646→   646→The context
   647→   647→
   648→   648→Reeves 2020, Bastalich and McCulloch 2022, Hillebrand and Leysinger 2023).
   649→   649→If the project goes wrong or falls behind schedule, candidates are much more
   650→   650→likely to look towards their supervisor for assistance and, if this is deemed to
   651→   651→be inadequate, to take matters further. Supervisors then need to pay particular
   652→   652→attention to developing and sustaining productive relationships with candidates
   653→   653→as outlined in Chapter 6 and the development of timelines and milestones set
   654→   654→out in Chapter 11.
   655→   655→McDonaldisation
   656→   656→Historically, neither institutions nor research sponsors took much interest in whe­
   657→   657→ther doctoral candidates completed their degrees and, if so, how long it was taking
   658→   658→them. But during the third quarter of the 20th century, governments began
   659→   659→enquiring more closely about what they were getting in return for funding doc­
   660→   660→toral research, and the answers, at least in their view, were unsatisfactory. Studies
   661→   661→of completion rates in Western Europe (see Sadak 2004), the US (Golde 2005),
   662→   662→Canada (Elgar and Klein 2004), and Australia (Bourke, Hattie, and Anderson
   663→   663→2004) all suggested that around one half of doctoral students failed to gain their
   664→   664→degrees and, of those who did, relatively few completed within the allotted time.
   665→   665→In response, many governments and research sponsors resorted to what amoun­
   666→   666→ted, in one form or another, to compulsion.
   667→   667→Such compulsion forced institutions to act to improve submission and qualiﬁ­
   668→   668→cation rates, which in turn led to pressures on supervisors to ensure that candidates
   669→   669→submit and qualify within the allotted time span (see Soin and Huber 2021).
   670→   670→Issues and strategies which supervisors might adopt such as the introduction of
   671→   671→explicit milestones relating to progress are outlined in Chapter 11.
   672→   672→Regulation
   673→   673→Historically, neither research sponsors nor institutions enquired too closely about
   674→   674→what went on in doctoral education generally or with regard to supervision in
   675→   675→particular. However, in recent years concerns about the quality of the student
   676→   676→experience and completion rates and times have led to the widespread introduc­
   677→   677→tion of regulation. A number of countries now have national codes of practice
   678→   678→which are replicated at institutional level or institutions have developed their own
   679→   679→codes (see Bitusikova et al. 2010, Byrne, Jorgensen, and Loukkola 2013, Andres
   680→   680→et al. 2015, Nerad 2020).
   681→   681→So supervision has become a heavily regulated area of academic practice (see
   682→   682→Guccione 2018, Hasgall, Saenan, and Borrell-Damian 2019), and it has become
   683→   683→important that supervisors are aware of all of the relevant institutional rules and
   684→   684→regulations, as discussed in Chapters 2, 5, 6, 8 11, and 19.
   685→   685→A further development with regard to regulation has been in relation to the con­
   686→   686→duct of supervisors with their candidates. Supervisory relationships are by their very
   687→   687→nature unequal, with the supervisor being in a position of power over candidates,

   696→   696→Spencer 2021), and sexual harassment (see for example Bloom et al. 2021).

   701→   701→Collectivisation
   702→   702→The historical model of supervision has been one of research students having a
   703→   703→single supervisor. However, in recent years in many countries there have been
   704→   704→moves to collectivise supervision by establishing supervisory teams (see Kalman
   705→   705→et al. 2022, Wald, Kumar, and Sanderson 2022). While this can yield advan­
   706→   706→tages, it can also cause problems in so far as the relationship between super­
   707→   707→visors and students becomes more diffused and potentially subject to conﬂict
   708→   708→over competing intellectual perspectives, interpretations of projects, personal­
   709→   709→ities, supervisory roles, and supervisory styles. These issues are discussed further
   710→   710→in Chapter 7.
   711→   711→Structuration
   712→   712→Originally, doctoral education took the form of individual study; the supervisor
   713→   713→provided guidance, the candidate worked independently on his or her own.
   714→   714→While that largely remains the case in some countries even today, in many others
   715→   715→the desire to provide better training for researchers, improve submission and
   716→   716→completion times, and enhance employability has led institutions to establish
   717→   717→appropriate structures for doctoral studies. These include various graduate
   718→   718→schools, doctoral colleges, doctoral research schools, and doctoral training cen­
   719→   719→tres and partnerships (see Hasgall, Saenan, and Borrell-Damian 2019, Kovacevic,
   720→   720→Bitusikova, and Dagen2022).
   721→   721→They have also led to a move toward more structured programmes of study. The
   722→   722→latter normally include a curriculum in the forms of a taught research training
   723→   723→component, rigorous monitoring procedures, and a generic skills component relat­
   724→   724→ing to employability (see Kiley 2014, Gonzalez-Ocampo et al. 2015, Elliot et al.
   725→   725→2020, European Universities Association/Council for Doctoral Education 2022).
   726→   726→In these ways, doctoral education has increasingly become structured at both
   727→   727→institutional and the programme levels (see Bitusikova 2010, Kiley 2011, Andres
   728→   728→et al. 2015, McGloin and Wynne 2015, Grant et al. 2022, McKenna and van
   729→   729→Schalwyk 2023). Again, this means that supervisors need not only to understand
   730→   730→the disciplinary context of doctoral studies (Chapter 3) but the institutional ones
   731→   731→(Chapter 2) and programme ones (Chapter 4) as well.

   737→   737→Growth and diversiﬁcation of the candidate population
   738→   738→Massiﬁcation
   739→   739→Up to a few years ago, doctoral candidates were relatively few in number. But over
   740→   740→the past two decades the numbers registering for doctoral programmes has grown
   741→   741→rapidly across most of the globe (Pederson 2014, Andres et al. 2015, Bao, Kehm, and
   742→   742→Ma 2016, Huang 2017, Shin, Kehm, and Jones 2018, Sarrico 2022). However, the
   743→   743→number of supervisors has not increased pro rata, with the result that supervisors have
   744→   744→more candidates to supervise. In this context, it is important that they are aware of
   745→   745→institutional policies relating to supervisory workloads and limits on candidate num­
   746→   746→bers (see Chapter 2) and of strategies for group supervision (see Chapter 6).
   747→   747→Domestic diversiﬁcation
   748→   748→Moreover, this expansion in numbers has been associated with an increase in the
   749→   749→diversity of the domestic graduate population Traditionally the stereotype doctoral
   750→   750→candidate was male, young, single, white, heterosexual, and without a disability.
   751→   751→Now, in many countries a majority of candidates are female, signiﬁcant numbers are
   752→   752→in their thirties or forties or older and have partners and families. While parity is a
   753→   753→long way off, in many cases there has been an increasing representation of indigen­
   754→   754→ous populations and ethnic and racial minority groups. Also, candidates have
   755→   755→become more diverse in their sexuality, and more have reported disabilities. The
   756→   756→needs of such candidates have come sharply into focus in recent years, and the
   757→   757→implications for their supervisory practice are discussed further in Chapters 5 and 15.
   758→   758→International diversiﬁcation
   759→   759→A further trend has been the internationalisation of doctoral education in the sense of
   760→   760→candidates studying for their doctorates in a country other than that of their origin.
   761→   761→While there is nothing new in this in principle – for example in the 19th century many
   762→   762→US and UK chemists did their doctoral studies in Germany (Simpson, 1983) – what has
   763→   763→changed very rapidly in recent years is a large-scale movement to study abroad for doc­
   764→   764→torates, particularly from the Southern to the Northern hemisphere. While this trend
   765→   765→was halted or reversed during the Covid-19 pandemic, subsequently it seems to have
   766→   766→resumed (de Wit and Altbach 2022) and signiﬁcant proportions of doctoral students are
   767→   767→now from countries other than the one in which they are studying for their degrees.
   768→   768→They bring huge beneﬁts to their host country, but may face challenges in their doctoral
   769→   769→studies which supervisors can help them overcome, as outlined in Chapter 16.

   772→   772→responsible for their own wellbeing and mental health. However, in recent years a

   789→   789→Diversiﬁcation of modes of study
   790→   790→Casualisation
   791→   791→As Taylor (2012: 121) has noted, ‘Historically, the model has been for doctoral
   792→   792→students to be full-time. While this largely remains the case in the natural sciences,
   793→   793→the picture can be very different in the arts, humanities, and social sciences where
   794→   794→part-time study is becoming much more common’. As a result, part-timers have
   795→   795→come to comprise a signiﬁcant proportion of the candidate population in a
   796→   796→number of countries. They are often older with family and employment commit­
   797→   797→ments which can affect their studies and of which supervisors need to be aware.
   798→   798→Issues in relation to supervising part-time candidates are discussed in Chapter 17.
   799→   799→Dislocation
   800→   800→Traditionally, doctoral candidates studied on campus, but the advances in infor­
   801→   801→mation and communications technology have meant that they can study at a
   802→   802→considerable distance from the host institution within a country, or even in
   803→   803→another country. In such cases, face-to-face contact between supervisors and stu­
   804→   804→dents may be infrequent and replaced by telephonic and electronic communica­
   805→   805→tion. The use of such media to supervise doctoral candidates was growing before
   806→   806→the Covid pandemic, but during the latter online supervision became the norm, as
   807→   807→did online examination. These developments have left legacies for supervisors
   808→   808→which are explored in Chapters 17 and 19.

   812→   812→tackling many of the major problems and issues in contemporary research often
   813→   813→involves the cross-fertilisation of insights from two or more disciplines (see Rashid

   822→   822→supervision are discussed in Chapter 3.
   823→   823→Proliferation
   824→   824→Whereas the PhD has been the dominant research degree for the past two cen­
   825→   825→turies, in the last two decades other forms of doctoral degrees have been devel­
   826→   826→oped, including doctorates by publication, practice-led doctorates, professional
   827→   827→doctorates, collaborative doctorates, and hybrids involving two or more of these
   828→   828→variants. The implications of these developments for supervision are considered in
   829→   829→Chapters 4 and 7.
   830→   830→Capitalisation
   831→   831→Historically again, the primary purpose of the doctorate has been to reproduce the
