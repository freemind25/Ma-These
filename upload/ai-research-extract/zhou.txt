This book series focuses on using artificial intelligence (AI) in educational contexts,
providing the venue to showcase the latest developments in this rapidly moving
field. From personalised instruction to adaptive assessments and immersive learning
experiences, AI tools and technologies can revolutionise education and empower
educators, students, and decision makers.
A wide range of topics surrounding the use of AI in learning and teaching are
explored, covering global perspectives from different communities and disciplines.
The book series approaches the use of AI in education with caution and addresses
ethical considerations such as data privacy, algorithmic bias, and transparency. By
examining AI socially responsible and inclusively, AI’s full potential shall be
unlocked and create a future of education that is personalised, engaging, and
equitable for all learners.
The titles in the series cover the use of AI within K-12 through tertiary settings
and in other domains such as professional, lifelong, and work-integrated learning.
The series accepts monographs and edited volumes that fit the scope mentioned
above and covers various topics, including (but not limited to) tutoring systems,
adaptive learning, feedback provision, learner modelling, assessment analytics, as
well as teaching and learning support. The series also includes titles covering related
issues of ethical and responsible use of AI, data privacy, bias and transparency,
educational design, institutional adoption, and inclusion.

Preface

In the rapidly evolving landscape of higher education, artificial intelligence (AI) has
emerged as a transformative force. This has significantly reshaped the way we
approach academic research. As students embark on their academic journey, it is
common for them to find themselves at the forefront of this technological revolution, attempting to take advantage of AI to enhance their research capabilities and
contribute to the advancement of knowledge in their chosen fields.
This book, Institutional Guide to Using AI for Research, is designed to provide a
general perspective on how higher education institutions (HEIs) could navigate the
exciting yet complex world of AI in research. Our aim is to equip HEIs with the
knowledge, skills, and ethical framework necessary to leverage AI tools (e.g., generative AI) effectively and responsibly in their academic pursuits. The integration of
AI into academic research is not merely a trend; it represents a fundamental shift in
how we process information, analyse data, and generate insights. From natural language processing algorithms that can sift through vast amounts of literature in seconds to machine learning models that can identify patterns in complex datasets,
generative AI is expanding the boundaries of what is possible in research across all
disciplines. Yet, this also brings a number of challenges and concerns among both
educators and students, with great power comes great responsibility. As we delve
into the applications of AI in research, we must also grapple with the ethical implications and potential pitfalls of relying too heavily on these technologies. This book
seeks to strike a balance between embracing the potential of AI and maintaining the
critical thinking and human insight that are at the core of academic inquiry.
Throughout this guide, we will explore the multifaceted role of AI in academic
research, providing you with both theoretical understanding and practical skills.

Abstract This chapter introduces the transformative role of Artificial Intelligence
(AI) in reshaping research methodologies across various academic fields. We delve
into fundamental AI concepts, its evolution, its growing significance in academic
research, and its impact on enhancing research methodologies. As AI technologies
become increasingly integral to the research process—from literature review and
data collection to analysis and hypothesis generation—students, academics, and
professionals must grasp the profound implications and opportunities AI presents.
This chapter also addresses the critical challenges of data privacy, algorithmic bias,
and the interpretability of AI-generated results, which are paramount in ensuring
responsible and ethical research. Through a comprehensive overview, this chapter
equips students with the necessary knowledge and critical thinking skills to navigate
the complex landscape of AI in research. We explore the impact of AI on developing
research skills, the ethical considerations in its application, and future trends in
AI-driven research. By the end of this chapter, students will better understand AI’s
role in modern research, enabling them to critically evaluate the benefits and limitations of AI tools in their respective fields.

2 AI in Research
AI generally refers to computer-controlled machines that perform tasks similar to
those carried out by humans. In research, it could refer to the tools, skills, and practices that are commonly used by students to understand aspects related to their
research. Generative AI (GenAI) is the latest form of AI that generates contents in
response to users in the form of text, images, or audio using a large training data.
ChatGPT and Dall-E are an example of GenAI that serves. These tools are extensively used in the higher education sector to support various stages of research
development, but their use, value and challenges for the research community keeps
on changing. This is because AI keeps on evolving as a result of increased in individuals’ digital footprint and usage. This increase has resulted in reforming the way
AI learn and respond to diverse needs of individuals.

This chapter shades the light on the potential of GenAI in research workflow.
Figure 1.1 break down the stages of where GenAI has been reported in the literature
to be used in research practices. From the Figure, it can be seen that GenAI play a
role in supporting researchers at an early stage of their research. In the proposal
stage, students have been widely considered to take advantage of GenAI to ensure
their ideas and narratives are presented in academic manners. For example, GenAI
has been reported to offer the means for students to create a structure for their
research proposal and help draft parts or refine or edit language of it (Andersen
et al., 2024). Ideation, synthesis of previous studies, reflection, and writing were the
main topics discussed by previous studies at the proposal writing stage with the support of GenAI. A significant aspect of this viewpoint was the potential of generative
tools to enhance students’ idea generation and writing skills by providing suggestions and offering feedback on grammar, sentence structure, and word choice
(Al-Samarraie et al., 2024). It is worth noting that the inclination to use GenAI tools
at the proposal stage might stem from the ability of these tools to drive motivation,
flexibility, and competency among users of specific learning needs.
Our review of the literature, seeding activities (university wide GenAI brainstorming sessions), and conversations with key educational organizations in the UK
have led to the understanding that GenAI can be widely used to:
1. Identify the need for knowledge and clarify ethical considerations.
2. Collect relevant information and generate data following appropriate
methodologies.
3. Evaluate information/data and reflect on the processes used.
4. Organise information and manage research stages.
5. Analyse information/data and synthesize new knowledge to produce coherent
understandings.
6. Communicate the processes, understandings, and applications of the research,
and respond to feedback while being mindful of ethical, cultural, and social issues.
Literature writing, data analysis, results interpretation, and reporting have been recognised as key research practices where students relay on GenAI tools to progress
in their research. These applications of GenAI vary significantly across disciplines,
which is largely linked to the choice of methodology adopted in each field to seek
new knowledge. For example, in the humanities, students may employ GenAI to
refine language and structure in their academic writing, while in the sciences, they
may rely on AI for complex data analysis and simulation modelling. In both cases,
the use of GenAI has been found to help—to some level—streamline research processes by offering quick access to relevant information, generating data insights,
and ensuring clarity and coherence of findings.
Yet, to mitigate the potential misuse of GenAI tools in forming irrelevant or inaccurate research directions, many doctoral colleges across the sector have acknowledged the lack of unified policies to clearly define what GenAI tools are acceptable
for use in research writing and how they should be implemented. In addition, there
is a significant gap in available training resources to guide students and staff in
effectively and ethically using these technologies. Such lack has been perceived by

3 Bibliometric Analysis of GenAI in Research
To explore the evolving nature of GenAI, we conducted a bibliometric analysis of
studies focusing on its use in research. Using R, we analysed and visualised trends,
sentiments, and topics from research articles retrieved from the Scopus database
using keywords that reflect GenAI and research practices. We began by creating a
.bib file containing 2520 studies, including details such as authors, titles, keywords,
abstracts, and publication years. This .bib file was then imported into R for topic
modelling to identify key terms in the literature. Several preprocessing steps were
carried out in R to clean and normalise the text data. From there, we used a Latent
Dirichlet Allocation model to extract a document-topic matrix, helping us understand how topics were distributed across the documents. We visualised the top terms
for each topic. We also analysed the sentiments expressed in the abstracts of these
studies, which were extracted and visualised. Figure 1.2 illustrates the volume of

using GenAI tools for idea generation, planning of the literature, identify research

5 How AI Is Supporting Research Skills Development?
AI is transforming how researchers approach their work, from literature review, and
hypothesis generation to data analysis. Khalifa and Albadawy (2024) carried out a
systematic review to examine how AI is utilised and its impact on academic writing
and research. Their research revealed that AI has improved academic writing and
research across six key areas: idea generation, content structuring, literature synthesis, data management, editing, and ethical compliance, as illustrated in Fig. 1.6.
Both UNESCO (2023) and the European Commission (2024) have acknowledged
the growing trend of using General AI to aid academic research. They have provided
detailed guidelines to support academics in effectively utilising GenAI for research
purposes. In the following, we delved into the specific ways AI is fostering research
skills development.

5.1 Facilitation of Literature Review
and Information Synthesis
One of the most significant contributions of AI to research skills development is in
the area of literature review and information synthesis. The exponential growth of
scientific publications makes it increasingly challenging for researchers to stay
abreast of developments in their field. AI-powered tools are helping to address this
challenge, while also fostering important research skills (Wagner et al., 2022).
Natural Language Processing (NLP) algorithms can rapidly analyse vast numbers of scientific papers, extracting key information, identifying trends, and even
suggesting connections between seemingly disparate studies. These tools are not
just saving time; they’re changing how researchers approach the literature review
process. AI tools that blend large language models (LLMs) with academic databases, such as Elicit and Semantic Scholar, are extensively utilised to assist researchers in finding relevant literature. Some conversational AI tools, such as the GPT-4
model, allow for the use of retrieval-augmented LLMs that access external documents, thereby enhancing accuracy and reducing factual errors (Bubeck et al.,
2023). AI-driven literature review tools such as Connected Papers, Inciteful and
Research Rabbit can generate knowledge graphs, visually representing the relationships between different concepts, authors, and publications in a field (Aaron, 2021).

5.3 Assistance in Research Design and Hypothesis Generation
AI is also playing an increasingly important role in research design and hypothesis
generation, two critical aspects of the research process. By analysing patterns in
existing research and combining elements from different studies, some advanced AI
systems can suggest research questions and novel hypotheses for researchers to
explore (Alshater, 2022). For instance, in drug discovery research, AI algorithms
can analyse data on molecular structures, biological pathways, and existing drugs to
propose new compounds that might be effective against a particular disease (Chen
et al., 2023).
While the researcher still needs to evaluate these suggestions critically, this
AI-assisted approach is fostering more creative and wide-ranging hypothesis generation. In terms of experimental design, AI tools can help researchers optimise
their methodologies. Machine learning algorithms can simulate numerous experimental scenarios, helping researchers identify the most promising approaches
before they even step into the lab (Ma et al., 2024). This capability is particularly
valuable in fields where experiments are costly or time-consuming, such as particle
physics or clinical trials. Moreover, the latest GenAI tools can generate preliminary
research ideas, methodologies, and expected outcomes with simple prompts like
“Based on the research gap, generate 10 topical research questions for topic X”
(UNESCO, 2023). These tools provide foundational concepts that can inform the
overall research plan.
By engaging with these AI tools, researchers are developing more sophisticated
skills in hypothesis formulation and experimental planning. They’re learning to
think more systematically about the variables in their experiments and to consider a
wider range of possibilities in their hypotheses. Moreover, the process of critically
evaluating AI-generated hypotheses and experimental designs is itself fostering
important research skills. Researchers are developing a more nuanced understanding of what makes a good hypothesis or a well-designed experiment, as they learn
to discern the strengths and weaknesses of AI-generated suggestions.

While AI in academic writing is often associated with automating tasks like grammar checking and citation formatting, its true impact lies in fostering higher-order
writing skills such as critical analysis, argumentation, and stylistic refinement
(Khalifa & Albadawy, 2024). This might seem counterintuitive, but it stems from
how AI is transforming the nature of academic writing. As AI systems take over

Furthermore, the use of AI writing tools like Grammarly, which specialises in

6 Responsible AI Integration
The integration of AI into research processes offers unprecedented opportunities for
advancing knowledge and accelerating scientific discovery. However, this integration also brings some ethical considerations and practical challenges that researchers must understand. The followings are the main considerations.

6.1 Ethical Considerations
• Data Privacy:
• One of the foremost ethical concerns in AI-driven research is data privacy. AI
systems often require vast amounts of data to function effectively, and in many
research contexts, this data may include sensitive personal information.
Researchers must grapple with questions of data ownership, consent, and the
potential for re-identification of anonymised data. The challenge lies in balancing the need for comprehensive datasets with the ethical imperative to protect
individual privacy. Techniques such as differential privacy and federated learning
are emerging as potential solutions, allowing researchers to gain insights from
data without directly accessing individual records (UNESCO, 2023). However,
implementing these techniques can be complex and may impact the accuracy of
results, presenting researchers with difficult trade-offs. Moreover, as AI systems
become more sophisticated, there’s an increasing risk of unintended data disclosures. For instance, an AI model trained on anonymised data might inadvertently
learn patterns that could lead to re-identification of individuals. Researchers
must be vigilant about these risks and implement robust safeguards to protect
privacy throughout the research process (Perkins & Roe, 2024a).
• Bias in AI Algorithms:
• Another critical ethical consideration is the potential for bias in AI algorithms.
AI systems learn from the data they’re trained on, and if this data contains historical biases or is not representative of diverse populations, the resulting algorithms can perpetuate or even amplify these biases (Hacker et al., 2024). In
research contexts, biased AI systems can lead to skewed results, misrepresentation of certain groups, and ultimately, flawed conclusions (Afrin et al., 2022).
This is particularly concerning in fields like healthcare, social sciences, and policy research, where biased outcomes could have far-reaching societal implications. Addressing algorithmic bias requires a multifaceted approach. Researchers
must critically examine their data sources, actively seek diverse and representative datasets, and implement bias detection and mitigation techniques in their AI
models. However, identifying and correcting for all potential biases is a complex
task that requires ongoing vigilance and interdisciplinary collaboration.

• Transparency and Explainability:
• The “black box” nature of many advanced AI systems, particularly deep learning
models, poses significant challenges for transparency in research. When AI is
used to analyse data or generate insights, it’s crucial that researchers understand
and explain how these results were obtained. The lack of explainability in AI
systems or tools can undermine the scientific principle of reproducibility and
make it difficult to validate research findings. Furthermore, in fields where AI-­
driven decisions have significant consequences, such as medical diagnosis or
criminal justice research, the inability to explain how an AI reached its conclusion raises serious ethical concerns.

6.2 Responsible AI Integration in Research
AI techniques and tools are evolving at an unprecedented rate, making it difficult for
researchers, especially those without a background in computer science, to stay
informed about the latest developments and best practices. This rapid evolution also
means that ethical guidelines and regulatory frameworks often lag behind technological capabilities (UNESCO, 2023). Researchers may find themselves in unexplored ethical territory, having to make decisions about the use of AI technologies
before clear guidelines have been established. To address this challenge, there’s a
growing need for ongoing education and interdisciplinary collaboration. Researchers
need access to resources and training that can help them understand the ethical
implications of new AI technologies and how to implement them responsibly
(Perkins & Roe, 2024b).
• Balancing AI Capabilities with Human Expertise:
• Another significant challenge lies in striking the right balance between leveraging AI capabilities and maintaining the critical role of human expertise in
research. While AI can process vast amounts of data and identify patterns beyond
human capability, it lacks the nuanced understanding, creativity, and ethical
judgment that human researchers bring to the table. Solid research continues to
depend on researchers’ deep subject knowledge, thorough analysis, and interpretation. AI cannot substitute human intelligence when it comes to critical thinking
and the creation of new knowledge. There’s a risk that over-reliance on AI could
lead to a devaluation of human expertise or a reluctance to question AI-generated
results. This may particularly affect junior researchers by depriving them of valuable learning experiences through trial and error. Researchers must cultivate a
mindset that views AI as a tool to augment human capabilities rather than replace
them. This requires developing new skills in interpreting and critically evaluating
AI outputs, as well as designing research methodologies that effectively combine
AI and human insights (European Commission, 2024).

• Ensuring Equitable Access and Preventing AI Divide:
• As AI becomes increasingly integral to cutting-edge research, there is a risk of
exacerbating existing inequalities in the research landscape. Advanced AI systems often require significant computational resources and technical expertise,
which may not be equally available to all researchers or institutions. Additionally,
researchers who have access to more advanced versions of GenAI, such as
ChatGPT 4, often experience enhanced productivity and effectiveness. This
potential “AI divide” could lead to a concentration of AI-driven research capabilities in well-funded institutions or technologically advanced countries, potentially skewing the direction of global research and limiting diverse perspectives.
Addressing this challenge requires concerted efforts to democratise access to AI
technologies in research. This might involve developing more accessible AI
tools, promoting open-source AI initiatives, and investing in AI education and
infrastructure in underrepresented communities and developing countries.
In conclusion, responsible AI integration in research presents a complex landscape
of ethical considerations and practical challenges. Addressing issues of data privacy,
algorithmic bias, and transparency, while also navigating the rapid pace of technological change and ensuring equitable access, requires a concerted effort from the
entire research community. The key to responsible AI integration lies in maintaining
a balance—leveraging the power of AI to enhance research capabilities while steadfastly upholding ethical principles and the irreplaceable value of human insight and
oversight. As we continue to explore the frontiers of AI-driven research, ongoing
dialogue, interdisciplinary collaboration, and a commitment to ethical practice will
be crucial in realising the full potential of AI while safeguarding the integrity and
societal impact of research.

Efforts to support researchers’ independence in using AI are still in their early stages
in higher education. This is mainly due to the constantly changing nature of AI
technology, which has led to shifts in educational policies and practices. As a result,
there’s considerable uncertainty about how best to integrate AI tools into research.
To make the most of these developments, universities need to help researchers adapt
to and use GenAI in a way that’s both ethical and effective. There’s also a growing
interest among universities to define the role of AI in research development and
policy. However, there’s a noticeable gap between what students expect and what
institutions prioritise, particularly when it comes to research outcomes and employability. This disconnect has made things challenging for both students and their
supervisors, especially as timely supervision and mentoring are vital. The mismatch
between expectations has created confusion around AI research practices, which
can lead to misunderstandings and knowledge gaps. This situation can negatively
impact students’ research results and their ability to meet institutional objectives.
