Over many years, I have guided students in their thesis work. As a
supervisor for over 10 PhD candidates and 100 bachelor’s and master’s theses, and as a reviewer or examiner for more than 1000 theses, I have witnessed the challenges the students have faced. I have
seen them wrestling with the formulations of their research questions,
struggling with their literature reviews, sweating over their data collection and data analysis, and grappling with their writing. Yet, I
have also seen all this hard work bearing fruit in well-crafted theses
with original, significant findings, making them the pinnacle of the
students’ academic work. Generative AI is promising, or threatening,
to change all of this.
Generative AI tools, such as ChatGPT, will be able to support all
parts and aspects of thesis writing. Students will have access to multitudes of virtual, but devoted and indefatigable, experts, research
assistants, supervisors, mentors, and editors. Will these tools take out
the drudgery in thesis writing and leave the fun and creative parts?
Will they enable students to produce theses with higher quality, and
learn more in the process, than ever before? Or will they lure the students into soulless and automated writing with predictable and dull
results? And what will the role of human supervisors be in this new
landscape?
This book does not answer these questions. Instead, it takes a more
practical approach by providing instruments, in the form of guidelines and prompts, that students and supervisors can use to act on
these questions — the book is rather about changing than explaining.
The instruments encourage students to work with generative AI in an
iterative, open-ended, creative and critical way instead of just making
the writing more efficient. While generative AI is here to stay as a vital part of the thesis writing process, it is up to students and teachers
to form its use.
When writing the book, I have made extensive use of ChatGPT and
Grammarly Go both for suggesting and editing text. Some prompts
that I have used repeatedly can be found in Chapters 13 and 14. Furthermore, the chapter illustrations have all been generated with Midjourney, using the word “steampunk”, as a metaphor for the craft of
thesis writing infused with the new technology of generative AI and
the art of prompting. The book was typeset in LATEX using the template “A Classic Thesis Style” by André Miede. And ChatGPT has
proved a valuable resource for making up for my limited knowledge
of LATEX.
I have learnt much about generative AI when writing this book, in
particular, that you can often get both useful and surprising results
from it if you just prompt a little better. My hope is that the reader
will find the book both helpful and inspiring, not only as a step towards better thesis writing, but also as a source for reflecting about

A thesis is the culminating work of your degree, be it a bachelor, a
master or a PhD. It will demonstrate your expertise and interest in a
particular subject within your field of study. A thesis is also a valuable
learning experience in itself that will prepare you for academic work,
professional research roles as well as other career paths.
1.1

the purpose of writing a thesis

Writing a thesis serves a practical purpose, as it is often a requirement
for obtaining a degree. However, there are additional and deeper benefits that go beyond this. By writing a thesis, you develop skills in
generating, exploring, synthesizing, and communicating knowledge
and ideas.
Creating Knowledge Through Research. Writing a thesis will teach
you how to conduct systematic investigations to discover new knowledge or affirm known truths. It provides hands-on experience in designing and implementing research methods, such as experiments,
case studies, interviews, and data analysis. You will gain proficiency
in managing projects, setting goals, achieving results, and meeting
deadlines. You will learn about attention to detail, technical skills,
problem-solving as well as patience and persistence. In other words,
by writing a thesis, you will learn how to become a researcher.
Exploring and Synthesizing Through Scholarship. By writing a
thesis, you will learn to delve deeply into a topic, which is usually
not possible during ordinary coursework. You will experience how to
learn from different sources, in particular academic articles, to gather

diverse ideas and perspectives. You will come to see the relationships
between your thesis and existing knowledge in the field. You will
learn to discuss and defend your work, both with your supervisor
and fellow students. You will come to understand how critical thinking, curiosity, open-mindedness, and humility can help to enhance
your thesis. In other words, by writing a thesis, you will learn how to
become a scholar.
Communicating Through Academic Writing. By writing a thesis,
you will learn how to communicate with an audience through the
written word. You will acquire the skill to express complex thoughts
with clarity and precision. You will learn to write coherently and concisely, ensuring that your text flows logically without being verbose.
And you will learn how to structure a long academic text in a readerfriendly manner. In other words, by writing a thesis, you will learn
how to become an academic writer.
An analogy can help to clarify the differences between a scholar, a researcher and an academic writer. A scholar is like someone who has a
vast collection of LEGO sets. They have studied each set’s instructions
closely and can explain how various pieces and sets connect. They appreciate the designs others have made and can tell you all about them.
They love to learn, discuss, and share their knowledge about these
LEGO sets. In contrast, a researcher is like a LEGO enthusiast who
doesn’t just stick to the instructions. Instead, they experiment with
the bricks, trying to create something new. They love to innovate and
discover new possibilities with the bricks. And an academic writer
is like the person who writes and illustrates the LEGO instruction
manuals. They take the knowledge from the LEGO collector (scholar)
and the innovations from the LEGO experimenter (researcher) and
create clear, step-by-step guides for others to follow. They are skilled
in presenting information in a clear, organized, and understandable
manner.
In the academic world, the roles of researcher, scholar, and academic writer are usually performed by the same individual. While
each role has its distinct characteristics, they should not be separated,
as the activities of inquiry, discovery, and dissemination contribute to
and enhance each other.
1.2

the imrad structure

Research findings can be presented in different ways, but using a standardized structure makes it easier for readers to follow the logic and
reasoning of the presentation. The IMRAD (Introduction, Method, Results And Discussion) structure has become an established format for
organising both research papers and theses in the natural as well as
social sciences. IMRAD suggests a division into four parts:
• Introduction — Why did you start the study? This section aims to
provide an overview of the research presented in the thesis. It

begins by providing a brief introduction to the area of study.
It then goes on to explain the research problem and formulate
research questions or goals. This section also provides a conceptual framework, explaining and discussing the key notions of
the thesis. Furthermore, it includes a literature review, summarizing existing research including current knowledge gaps.
• Method — What did you do and how? This section describes the
research strategies, methods of data collection, and data analysis that were employed, and explains how they were utilized. It
provides a rationale for the choice of these strategies and methods, and also addresses research ethics.
• Results — What did you find? This section answers the research
question of the thesis. It presents and explains the research results, thereby often including tables, graphs, charts, and figures
to represent the results visually.
• Discussion — What does it mean? This section presents a critical
analysis and interpretation of the research conducted and its
results. It highlights limitations that were encountered during
the study, compares the results to those of prior research, and
discusses the practical and theoretical implications of the outcomes. Furthermore, it outlines future research areas that could
be explored.

1.4

prompt engineering for thesis writing

Forms of Prompts
Prompts come in many forms and shapes, and some of the most common ones are listed below. These forms of prompts can be combined
in order to craft a prompt that conveys your intention and helps ChatGPT provide a focused and relevant answer.
Standard Prompts. Standard prompts are a simple way to guide
ChatGPT by providing a specific task for it to complete. Standard
prompts usually start with a word, such as “Write”, “Create” or “Gen-

1.4 prompt engineering for thesis writing

erate”, followed by a noun phrase. Standard prompts can be seen as
the basic form of prompts, which can be extended in various ways.
Some examples are:
• Write a review of this article
• Create themes for these responses
• Explain the process of photosynthesis
Instruction Prompts. Instruction prompts extend standard prompts
by providing explicit instructions on how ChatGPT should perform a
task. Providing detailed instructions allows ChatGPT to give relevant
answers that reflect your intentions. Some examples are:
• Write a review of this movie that focuses on the protagonist
• Create interview questions that are friendly and easy to understand
• Explain the process of photosynthesis, detailing the key stages,
the role of chlorophyll, and the importance of sunlight and carbon dioxide
Role Prompts. Role Prompts are prompts in which you ask ChatGPT
to take on a certain role, e.g a researcher, a student or a lawyer. Some
examples are:
• As a researcher, explain the process of photosynthesis
• As a journalist, explain the process of photosynthesis
• As a lawyer, explain GDPR
Audience Prompts. Audience prompts are prompts in which you ask
ChatGPT to provide answers that are suitable for a specific audience,
e.g. children, experts, or non-experts. Some examples are:
• Explain the process of photosynthesis for a biologist
• Explain the process of photosynthesis for a twelve-year-old child
• As a journalist, explain the process of photosynthesis for a biologist
Modifier Prompts. Modifier prompts are prompts in which a single
word or phrase, the modifier, completely changes the tone or meaning of the prompt, thereby causing ChatGPT to give a very different
answer. Modifiers are usually adjectives modifying nouns or adverbs
modifying verbs. Some examples are (with the modifier in italics):
• Propose an innovative design for a vacuum cleaner
• Write a popular summary of this article
• Write a scathing review of this movie that focuses on the protagonist

Contextual Prompts. Contextual prompts are prompts that include
the context in which you are prompting. It could, for example, be
some activity you are carrying out or some goal you want to achieve.
Some examples are (with the context in italics):
• My research question is, “What are the causes of eating disorders

Guidelines for Prompting
A general guideline for good prompting is that you should not only
use one-shot prompts, i.e. simple questions without any follow-up
questions. Instead, you benefit more from using complex questions
in a dialogue, where you can guide ChatGPT in multiple steps to
provide you with relevant and diverse information. Here are a few
concrete guidelines to help you engage in a dialogue with ChatGPT.
Be Patient and Iterative. If the answer you received was not what
you were expecting or hoping for, you can ask ChatGPT to generate
a new answer. Sometimes, even if you were initially satisfied with
the answer, a regenerated response can provide a more original or
nuanced answer. Additionally, you can try asking follow-up prompts
to get more information, for example:
• Can you elaborate on . . .
• I didn’t understand X, can you explain it differently?
Be Open to Exploration. Allow room for ChatGPT to provide original or alternative answers and even playful or whimsical ones. Use
open-ended prompts by asking how and why questions. Some examples are:
• How can you carry out . . .
• What are some potential solutions to . . .
• Can you explore different viewpoints on . . .
Rephrase and Refine. Experiment with different phrasings or details to improve response quality. Checking out the different forms of
prompts above can help in the experimentation, in particular contextual prompts. Here are some examples:

• “What is the difference between semi-structured and unstructured interviews?” can be rephrased and refined as “What are
the advantages of semi-structured interviews to unstructured
interviews in the context of interviewing experts?”.
• “What is the difference between generative AI and machine
learning?” can be rephrased as “In what ways does generative
AI vary from traditional machine learning practices?”.
Guide the Response Format. If you want the answer from ChatGPT
to be in a certain format, you can specify this in the prompt, for example:
• What is the difference between generative AI and machine learning? Answer in one paragraph with 100 - 125 words.
• What are the advantages of using ChatGPT for thesis writing?
Answer with a bullet list consisting of five items.
Be Goal Oriented. When prompting in ChatGPT, it is always important to have a clear goal in mind. Are you, in the role of a researcher,
looking for idea generation, suggestions for appropriate methods,
and critiques of your method choices? Or are you, in the role of a
scholar, trying to provide explanations of key notions, synthesize literature, and relate your work to previous research? Or do you, as
an academic writer, want to formulate complex thoughts with clarity,
improve coherence and conciseness, and identify ways to visualize
your results?
It is also useful to differentiate between prompts that aid in brainstorming and those that assist in evaluating the quality of your writing. Brainstorming prompts can help you generate creative ideas related to, for example, your research problem, research question, or
study design. They can also assist in drafting text, such as literature
summaries, interview questions, or ethical consent forms. In contrast,
evaluating prompts are designed to review text that is mostly finished, to ensure it is complete, clear, precise, and meets other quality
standards.

Generative AI is a branch of AI that can generate new data samples
that are statistically similar to a specific dataset. It has seen rapid
development and is now being used in various fields such as art,
literature, and healthcare. This chapter provides a brief overview of
generative AI by discussing its basic principles and methodologies,
diverse applications, and ethical considerations.
2.1

fundamental concepts and methodologies

Generative models are a type of artificial intelligence that specialize
in creating new content that resembles existing data. They work by
analyzing a given dataset and then generating new data that follows
a similar pattern. You can think of it like an artist who has spent
years practicing by copying the styles of famous painters. Once they
have mastered these styles, they can create new paintings that look
like they were made by the great masters themselves. In the same
way, generative models use existing data to create something new, but
instead of paintings, they create digital content. Generative models
learn by going through huge amounts of data, like photos or text.
They look for patterns and features that define the style or content of
this data. Once they understand these patterns, they can use them to
generate new, similar pieces of content. There are two main types of
generative models that are commonly used:
• Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) work like a talented forger who
tries to recreate a masterpiece. Such a forger begins by studying
the artwork to understand its basic structure and style. Once
they have analyzed the details, they try to create a new painting

12

that captures the essence of the original. Similarly, VAEs do this
with digital data. They learn to understand the structure of a
given dataset and then generate new instances that are similar
to it. By doing so, they can produce new content that resembles
the original data.
• Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) work like a game between two artists — one of them creates paintings, and the other
judges them. The creator, also known as the "generator," tries to
make paintings so good that the judge cannot tell if they are
originals or fakes. The judge, called the "discriminator," tries to
get better at distinguishing between the two. Over time, both
artists improve their skills, resulting in highly realistic digital
content. This technique is widely used in machine learning to
generate new content that is similar to some existing data.
2.2

applications of generative ai

Generative AI has been applied in various sectors of society, providing innovative solutions and opening up new opportunities. Below
are some of the key application areas:
Art and Creativity. Generative AI can create artwork, imitating
styles of famous artists or generating unique pieces. AI algorithms
can compose music in various genres, assisting or even leading the
creative process. Based on prompts, AI can generate poems, stories,
and other written content.
Entertainment and Media. For video games, AI can generate realistic environments, characters, and game elements. In film and animation, it can assist in animation, creating realistic visual effects, and
even generating scripts. And for virtual reality, it can create immersive and realistic virtual environments.
Education. AI can generate customized worksheets, quizzes, and
learning resources based on specific curriculum requirements and
student needs. It can offer real-time translation services to help nonnative speakers understand educational content. And it can create virtual labs for students to conduct experiments, providing a safe and
controlled environment.
Healthcare and Biotechnology. Generative models can suggest
new molecular structures for potential drugs, predicting which compounds might be most effective for specific diseases. It can analyze
patient data to recommend personalized treatment plans. It can automate the extraction of relevant information from unstructured EHR
data, and create concise patient summaries from extensive EHR data,
aiding clinicians in decision-making.

2.3 ethical considerations

Retail and E-commerce. AI can generate tailored product suggestions and descriptions for customers. It can assist in copywriting and
graphic design of advertisements, banners, and social media content.
It can also automate responses in customer service interactions.
Technology and IT. In software development, generative AI can
write code snippets and provide programming assistance. It can identify bugs in code and suggest fixes. In the area of cybersecurity, it can
create simulations of cyber threats for training and preparedness.
2.3

ethical considerations

Generative AI raises several ethical considerations that need to be
addressed to ensure responsible and fair use. Some of the major concerns are:
• Privacy and Consent. Ensuring that any data used to train generative models is properly anonymized to protect individuals’
privacy. Obtaining proper consent for the use of personal data
in training models.
• Intellectual Property. Addressing questions around the ownership of content generated by AI, especially in creative fields like
art, music, or writing. Determining how patent laws apply to
inventions or innovations generated by AI.
• Social Impact. Addressing the potential for generative AI to displace or degrade jobs, particularly in fields like content creation,
design, and customer service.
• Bias and Fairness. Generative AI models learn from data, and if
the data is biased, the output will reflect that bias. Ensuring fairness requires careful curation and monitoring of training data.
The generative models should not favour one group over another and be representative of diverse populations.
• Misuse and Malicious Use. Addressing the ethical implications of
using generative AI to create deepfakes, which can be used to
spread misinformation or harm individuals’ reputations.

In a thesis, the research problem is the central issue or challenge that
you aim to address through your research. It answers the why questions of your thesis: “Why did you decide to conduct your study in
the first place?” and “Why is your study valuable and important?”.
The research problem is usually broad in scope and may involve complex issues that require in-depth investigations. It frames your study
and motivates the choice of your research question.
A research problem can be described in three parts. First, a background offering contextual information that sets the stage for the research problem. Secondly, a clear, concise statement expressing the
research problem. Thirdly, a justification of the research problem, consisting of reasons why this problem is worth investigating, including
its relevance, originality, significance, and timeliness.

and testing them. Some examples of theoretical research problems

3.2

practical research problems

A practical research problem aims to solve real-world issues that need
workable solutions. Unlike theoretical research, which focuses on understanding and advancing theories, practical research looks for solutions to specific problems that people experience. The goal is to use
the research findings to make real-world improvements for real people in real environments. Practical research often involves not only
researchers but also stakeholders such as citizens, organisations, or
policymakers who are directly or indirectly affected by the research.
Some examples of practical research problems are:
• The rate of hospital-acquired infections is high and increasing
• There is a shortage of landfills that can be used to recycle household waste
• The use of learning platforms sometimes reduces student engagement and academic performance
Conducting practical research can serve a dual purpose. It not only
helps in resolving real-world issues but can sometimes contribute to
theoretical knowledge by providing evidence that either confirms, refutes, or refines existing theories. This evidence can also draw attention to gaps in current theories, thereby encouraging further theoretical development.

If you are delving into a complex and technical topic, your research
problem may seem somewhat dry or even downright boring. In such
a case, a scenario can help illustrate your research problem in an accessible, enjoyable and engaging way. Research problems are often
complex and multifaceted, but a well-crafted scenario can express
the essence of your problem as an inspiring story, making it easier to
understand. Scenarios can also offer a context in which the research
problem exists, helping to clarify why the problem is important and
whom it affects. And a compelling scenario can capture your audience’s attention, even if they are not experts in the field, making
them more interested in your research. Furthermore, a scenario can
highlight the ethical and social dimensions of your research problem,
showing how it fits into a larger picture.
