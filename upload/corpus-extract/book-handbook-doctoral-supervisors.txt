Introduction

In 2005, Routledge published the ﬁrst edition of A Handbook for Doctoral
Supervisors written by Stan Taylor and the late Nigel Beasley, to whose memory
this edition is dedicated. The book was well received and was widely used as a
textbook and a source for professional development by supervisors across the
globe. Over time, it became out of date and Routledge commissioned a second
edition written by Stan Taylor, Margaret Kiley, and Robin Humphrey which was
published in 2018.
It is a measure of the pace of change in doctoral education that, six years later,
there is a need for a further edition. This reﬂects the fact that, in the relatively
short space of time since the second edition was published, there have been sig­
niﬁcant developments. These include variously; a much stronger focus on ensuring
equality of opportunity in access, particularly for indigenous populations and other
ethnic and racial minorities; a greater emphasis on ‘respect’ in supervisory-candi­
date relationships; a move towards online supervision and online examination; an
increased realisation of the need to offer doctoral candidates appropriate personal
support during their studies, particularly in relation to their wellbeing and mental
health; and a heightened awareness of the importance of assisting them to prepare
them for careers outside as well as inside academia.
These developments pre-dated the Covid-19 outbreak of 2020–2022 but in
many cases their importance was dramatically ampliﬁed during the pandemic,
leaving a legacy that is still continuing. All of them have impacted the roles of
supervisors, particularly in terms of recruiting and selecting candidates, managing
working relationships, offering academic advice and support in the online envir­
onment, and providing personal, professional, and career support. These changes
have attracted a considerable amount of attention from researchers in the ﬁeld of
doctoral education, and this has been reﬂected in a rapid growth in published
research.
The aims of this new edition are to incorporate these changes in the roles of
supervisors into the content of the Handbook and to bring the latter up to date
with the latest research. This has involved a substantial re-writing of many of the
original chapters to incorporate new themes and new materials.

As with the ﬁrst and second editions, the third is aimed at the potential or

We are aware that, while there is no hard and fast rule, the term ‘supervisor’
tends to be used in most of Europe, the UK, and Oceania. Elsewhere, parti­
cularly in the United States and countries with systems based upon that model,
the more common term is ‘advisor’. We are also aware that this is not just a
matter of style but also of substance; ‘supervisor’ implies a wider range of
intervention with regard to the candidate and to the research project than
‘advisor’. But, while we have written this book for supervisors (and used the
term throughout) we believe that most, if not all, of it is also relevant to
advisors, that is, in covering the roles of the former we are also covering those
of the latter.
With regard to ‘supervision’, it may be noted that our use of this term is pri­
marily conﬁned to the supervision of the candidate’s research project. It does not
include any taught components, but we do think it is important that, particularly
in the case of more structured doctorates, supervisors are aware of the whole
doctoral programme in order that they can, where appropriate, advise candidates
how to build upon their previous studies.
With regard to ‘effective’, this could be taken as meaning simply getting the
candidate over the line to a doctorate. But we believe there is more to it than that
in terms of both ‘outcomes’ and ‘outputs’. With regard to the former, ‘effective­
ness’ means a high-quality submission, completion on time or as near as possible,
and dissemination to the subject community; with regard to the latter, it means
that the candidate has successfully developed the creative, critical, and analytical
skills and knowledge of a researcher and laid the basis for their future career inside,
or increasingly outside, academia.

Chapter 1

The overall context

Introduction
Supervisors tend to be immersed in their busy academic and/or professional lives,
not to mention their personal ones, and seldom have the time to maintain an
overview of ongoing developments and research in doctoral education. As a con­
sequence, many are experiencing new demands without necessarily understanding
the wider context within which they arise. The purpose of the present chapter is to
try to set that wider context. It is based on the work of Taylor (2012, 2023) and
begins with a brief summary of the historical origins and evolution of doctoral
education from its modern origins through to the 1980s and then looks at sub­
sequent developments that have transformed the ﬁeld and the context of
supervision.

The origins and evolution of doctoral education
Doctorates have their origins in the mediaeval universities when they were used
primarily as a means of licensing teachers in the professions of medicine, law, and
theology (Bernstein et al. 2014, Amaral and Carvalho 2020). This paradigm
remained dominant until, in the early 19th century, a number of eminent
Enlightenment thinkers in Prussia began to advocate a new kind of university, one
in which the core mission would be the creation of original knowledge and
understanding and in which research would be pre-eminent. These advocates
included Friedrich Schliermacher, Johann Fichte, and Wilhelm von Humboldt
(see Watson 2010).
It was von Humboldt who was able to implement this new model when, in
1809, he became Under-Secretary in the Ministry of the Interior and responsible
for education in Prussia. Although he was in ofﬁce for a mere 16 months, he
initiated reforms designed, as Nybom (2003: 152) has put it, ‘to create an entirely
new type of academic institutional order and a new type of academic’.
With regard to the former, he took the lead in reorganising the University of
Berlin which in 1810 became the world’s ﬁrst explicitly research-led university.
With regard to the latter, the new type of academic was to be engaged both in

extending knowledge and understanding (research) and transmitting it (educa­
tion/teaching). Under the new university’s statutes training for this role was pro­
vided by completing a new kind of doctorate to be awarded to a researcher who
had made a signiﬁcant and original contribution to knowledge and understanding
in the sciences (see Clark 2006). To signify its academic as opposed to professional
status, this doctorate was awarded in ‘Die mutter aller Wissenschaften’ (the
mother of all sciences), namely philosophy.
The DPhil., or as it subsequently became, the PhD, was intended for a handful
of the brightest and the best who would become the next generation of aca­
demics; it was based on a ‘master-apprentice’ model of delivery; supervision was,
as Park (2005: 15) has put it, viewed as a ‘secret garden or an activity that takes
place behind closed doors between consenting adults’; candidates had to complete
a research project, write it up in the form of a thesis, and defend it at an oral
examination; and because it involved the creation of new knowledge, completion
took as long as it took (Archbald 2011).
What subsequently became known as the Humboldtian model of the doctorate
(see Ash 2006) was copied during the 19th century in a few other countries,
including France and the Netherlands (see Bogle 2017) and Russia (see Smo­
lentseva 2007). But if elsewhere the universities of the Old World paid little atten­
tion to the new degree, it was embraced by those of the New World, the United
States. There, the ﬁrst doctorate was awarded by Yale in 1861, and the precedent
was quickly followed by Harvard, Michigan, and Pennsylvania. The Humboldtian
model was also copied in Japan, where the ﬁrst doctoral programme was established
in 1887 at the founding of what is now the University of Tokyo (Yamamoto 2020).
The next extension of the doctorate was during the First World War when it
became apparent that Germany’s war effort had beneﬁtted signiﬁcantly from the
strength of the country’s scientiﬁc base. For this reason, as well as concerns about
candidates seeking doctoral studies elsewhere, the wartime coalition in the UK
pressed universities to introduce the degree. The ﬁrst was awarded by Oxford in
1920 and by 1921 the doctorate had been adopted by all of the UK universities.
(Simpson 1983). In the mid-1920s it was introduced in Ireland (Share 2016) and
South Africa (Herman 2015). At the end of that decade, Mexico became the ﬁrst
Latin American country to adopt the degree (see Malo 2007) and in the 1930s
the ﬁrst doctorates were awarded in Brazil (see Ribeiro 2007). In New Zealand,
following the initial implementation of the doctorate in the 1920s, it was perma­
nently reinstated in 1944. After the Second World War, Australia adopted the new
degree and the ﬁrst doctorate was awarded in 1948 (Dobson 2012).
By the late 1940s, the PhD had spread throughout the Anglo-American world,
but it was still resisted in universities across much of Western Europe, which clung
on to their historical professional licentiates. However, the 1950s and 1960s saw
research rise to the top of the political agenda as a key to both economic growth
and defence capability, and the need to train researchers was reﬂected in a rapid
growth of PhD programmes in Western European countries, with the ﬁnal adop­
ters being Italy (1988) and Denmark (1989).

By the late 1980s, the PhD had conquered Western Europe, but not Eastern
Europe. In most of what was then the ‘Soviet bloc’, there were different arrange­
ments based on the USSR model of a two-step doctorate which could be taken inside
or outside the universities, for example in the Academies of Sciences, and in either
case was subject to state approval (see Connelly 2000). However, the withdrawal of
the Soviet Union after the ‘velvet revolutions’ at the end of the 1980s and start of the
1990s was followed in many, although not all, of the newly-liberated countries by a
re-organisation of graduate education and the establishment or re-establishment of
the PhD in the universities (see Smolentseva, Huisman, and Froumin 2018, Yekibay
et al. 2022).
Further adopters of the doctorate in the third quarter of the 20th century were
in Asia. In China, the ﬁrst formal programme began in 1978 with 12 doctoral
candidates; within 20 years there were over 50,000 (see Bao, Kehm, and Ma
2016, Huang 2017). In the 1980s and 1990s doctoral programmes were also
established or expanded in many other Asian countries, including Bangladesh,
India, Malaysia, Pakistan, Thailand, and Singapore (UNESCO 2014, Zhaung,
Liu, and Ding 2022).
Therefore, by the 1990s, the PhD had become a qualiﬁcation delivered over a
large proportion of the globe. In its essentials, it was recognisable as the degree which
had been introduced at the University of Berlin just under two centuries earlier.

Developments in the doctorate in the late 20th and 21st centuries
However, over the last three decades or so, as Hammond et al. (2010) have sug­
gested, the doctorate has been transformed by four major clusters of develop­
ments, namely formalisation, the growth and diversiﬁcation of the candidate
population, the diversiﬁcation of modes of study, and the diversiﬁcation of pur­
poses. Each of these can be disaggregated into a number of sub-headings.
Formalisation
Historically, doctoral education was largely left to supervisors and candidates with­
out external intervention. But it has now been formalised through the processes of
commodiﬁcation, McDonaldisation, regulation, collectivisation, and structuration.
Commodiﬁcation
As Taylor (2012: 122) notes: ‘The traditional model of the relationship between
students and supervisors was that of master and apprentice whereby the latter
learned by emulating the former.’ But, in the same way as undergraduate and
taught postgraduate education has been fashioned by neo-liberal government
policies into a ‘provider-consumer’ framework, so has doctoral education with
candidates being encouraged to expect their supervisors to offer a service in
supporting their research projects (see for example Denicolo, Duke, and

Reeves 2020, Bastalich and McCulloch 2022, Hillebrand and Leysinger 2023).
If the project goes wrong or falls behind schedule, candidates are much more
likely to look towards their supervisor for assistance and, if this is deemed to
be inadequate, to take matters further. Supervisors then need to pay particular
attention to developing and sustaining productive relationships with candidates
as outlined in Chapter 6 and the development of timelines and milestones set
out in Chapter 11.
McDonaldisation
Historically, neither institutions nor research sponsors took much interest in whe­
ther doctoral candidates completed their degrees and, if so, how long it was taking
them. But during the third quarter of the 20th century, governments began
enquiring more closely about what they were getting in return for funding doc­
toral research, and the answers, at least in their view, were unsatisfactory. Studies
of completion rates in Western Europe (see Sadak 2004), the US (Golde 2005),
Canada (Elgar and Klein 2004), and Australia (Bourke, Hattie, and Anderson
2004) all suggested that around one half of doctoral students failed to gain their
degrees and, of those who did, relatively few completed within the allotted time.
In response, many governments and research sponsors resorted to what amoun­
ted, in one form or another, to compulsion.
Such compulsion forced institutions to act to improve submission and qualiﬁ­
cation rates, which in turn led to pressures on supervisors to ensure that candidates
submit and qualify within the allotted time span (see Soin and Huber 2021).
Issues and strategies which supervisors might adopt such as the introduction of
explicit milestones relating to progress are outlined in Chapter 11.
Regulation
Historically, neither research sponsors nor institutions enquired too closely about
what went on in doctoral education generally or with regard to supervision in
particular. However, in recent years concerns about the quality of the student
experience and completion rates and times have led to the widespread introduc­
tion of regulation. A number of countries now have national codes of practice
which are replicated at institutional level or institutions have developed their own
codes (see Bitusikova et al. 2010, Byrne, Jorgensen, and Loukkola 2013, Andres
et al. 2015, Nerad 2020).
So supervision has become a heavily regulated area of academic practice (see
Guccione 2018, Hasgall, Saenan, and Borrell-Damian 2019), and it has become
important that supervisors are aware of all of the relevant institutional rules and
regulations, as discussed in Chapters 2, 5, 6, 8 11, and 19.
A further development with regard to regulation has been in relation to the con­
duct of supervisors with their candidates. Supervisory relationships are by their very
nature unequal, with the supervisor being in a position of power over candidates,

11

which the vast majority exercise with due restraint and respect. But some have abused
their position and behaved in ways that are unacceptable, including bullying (see for
example English, Flaherty, and English 2018), discrimination (see for example
Spencer 2021), and sexual harassment (see for example Bloom et al. 2021).
While for many years such misconduct was brushed under the carpet, in recent
years it has been exposed and in some countries national bodies and/or institu­
tions have issued codes of conduct for supervisors, of which the latter need to be
aware. These matters are discussed in Chapters 6, 15 and 16.
Collectivisation
The historical model of supervision has been one of research students having a
single supervisor. However, in recent years in many countries there have been
moves to collectivise supervision by establishing supervisory teams (see Kalman
et al. 2022, Wald, Kumar, and Sanderson 2022). While this can yield advan­
tages, it can also cause problems in so far as the relationship between super­
visors and students becomes more diffused and potentially subject to conﬂict
over competing intellectual perspectives, interpretations of projects, personal­
ities, supervisory roles, and supervisory styles. These issues are discussed further
in Chapter 7.
Structuration
Originally, doctoral education took the form of individual study; the supervisor
provided guidance, the candidate worked independently on his or her own.
While that largely remains the case in some countries even today, in many others
the desire to provide better training for researchers, improve submission and
completion times, and enhance employability has led institutions to establish
appropriate structures for doctoral studies. These include various graduate
schools, doctoral colleges, doctoral research schools, and doctoral training cen­
tres and partnerships (see Hasgall, Saenan, and Borrell-Damian 2019, Kovacevic,
Bitusikova, and Dagen2022).
They have also led to a move toward more structured programmes of study. The
latter normally include a curriculum in the forms of a taught research training
component, rigorous monitoring procedures, and a generic skills component relat­
ing to employability (see Kiley 2014, Gonzalez-Ocampo et al. 2015, Elliot et al.
2020, European Universities Association/Council for Doctoral Education 2022).
In these ways, doctoral education has increasingly become structured at both
institutional and the programme levels (see Bitusikova 2010, Kiley 2011, Andres
et al. 2015, McGloin and Wynne 2015, Grant et al. 2022, McKenna and van
Schalwyk 2023). Again, this means that supervisors need not only to understand
the disciplinary context of doctoral studies (Chapter 3) but the institutional ones
(Chapter 2) and programme ones (Chapter 4) as well.

Growth and diversiﬁcation of the candidate population
Massiﬁcation
Up to a few years ago, doctoral candidates were relatively few in number. But over
the past two decades the numbers registering for doctoral programmes has grown
rapidly across most of the globe (Pederson 2014, Andres et al. 2015, Bao, Kehm, and
Ma 2016, Huang 2017, Shin, Kehm, and Jones 2018, Sarrico 2022). However, the
number of supervisors has not increased pro rata, with the result that supervisors have
more candidates to supervise. In this context, it is important that they are aware of
institutional policies relating to supervisory workloads and limits on candidate num­
bers (see Chapter 2) and of strategies for group supervision (see Chapter 6).
Domestic diversiﬁcation
Moreover, this expansion in numbers has been associated with an increase in the
diversity of the domestic graduate population Traditionally the stereotype doctoral
candidate was male, young, single, white, heterosexual, and without a disability.
Now, in many countries a majority of candidates are female, signiﬁcant numbers are
in their thirties or forties or older and have partners and families. While parity is a
long way off, in many cases there has been an increasing representation of indigen­
ous populations and ethnic and racial minority groups. Also, candidates have
become more diverse in their sexuality, and more have reported disabilities. The
needs of such candidates have come sharply into focus in recent years, and the
implications for their supervisory practice are discussed further in Chapters 5 and 15.
International diversiﬁcation
A further trend has been the internationalisation of doctoral education in the sense of
candidates studying for their doctorates in a country other than that of their origin.
While there is nothing new in this in principle – for example in the 19th century many
US and UK chemists did their doctoral studies in Germany (Simpson, 1983) – what has
changed very rapidly in recent years is a large-scale movement to study abroad for doc­
torates, particularly from the Southern to the Northern hemisphere. While this trend
was halted or reversed during the Covid-19 pandemic, subsequently it seems to have
resumed (de Wit and Altbach 2022) and signiﬁcant proportions of doctoral students are
now from countries other than the one in which they are studying for their degrees.
They bring huge beneﬁts to their host country, but may face challenges in their doctoral
studies which supervisors can help them overcome, as outlined in Chapter 16.
Obligation
The traditional view was that, as doctoral candidates were adults, they should be
responsible for their own wellbeing and mental health. However, in recent years a

The overall context

13

substantial volume of evidence (see for example Levecque et al. 2017, Marais et al.
2017, Mackie and Bates 2018, Metcalfe, Wilson, and Levecque 2018) has accrued
which suggests that doctoral candidates are signiﬁcantly more likely than compar­
able groups of the population to suffer from low levels of wellbeing, mental dis­
tress (usually depression and anxiety), and mental illness (clinically-proven
depression and anxiety as well as more severe illnesses such as bi-polar and psy­
chosis). In view of these ﬁndings, institutions have had to accept that they have a
duty of care for the wellbeing and mental health of doctoral candidates. In part,
this has devolved upon supervisors as they are in personal contact with students
and hence most likely to be able to notice distress and direct candidates to sources
of support, as discussed in Chapter 12.
Diversiﬁcation of modes of study
Casualisation
As Taylor (2012: 121) has noted, ‘Historically, the model has been for doctoral
students to be full-time. While this largely remains the case in the natural sciences,
the picture can be very different in the arts, humanities, and social sciences where
part-time study is becoming much more common’. As a result, part-timers have
come to comprise a signiﬁcant proportion of the candidate population in a
number of countries. They are often older with family and employment commit­
ments which can affect their studies and of which supervisors need to be aware.
Issues in relation to supervising part-time candidates are discussed in Chapter 17.
Dislocation
Traditionally, doctoral candidates studied on campus, but the advances in infor­
mation and communications technology have meant that they can study at a
considerable distance from the host institution within a country, or even in
another country. In such cases, face-to-face contact between supervisors and stu­
dents may be infrequent and replaced by telephonic and electronic communica­
tion. The use of such media to supervise doctoral candidates was growing before
the Covid pandemic, but during the latter online supervision became the norm, as
did online examination. These developments have left legacies for supervisors
which are explored in Chapters 17 and 19.
Diversiﬁcation of purposes of study
Cross-fertilisation
Traditionally, doctorates have been undertaken within single disciplines. However,
tackling many of the major problems and issues in contemporary research often
involves the cross-fertilisation of insights from two or more disciplines (see Rashid

14

The context

2021), and hence doctoral research projects have become increasingly inter­
disciplinary. Where supervisors themselves have a mono-disciplinary pedigree,
operating across two or more can be challenging and the implications of this for
supervision are discussed in Chapter 3.
Proliferation
Whereas the PhD has been the dominant research degree for the past two cen­
turies, in the last two decades other forms of doctoral degrees have been devel­
oped, including doctorates by publication, practice-led doctorates, professional
doctorates, collaborative doctorates, and hybrids involving two or more of these
variants. The implications of these developments for supervision are considered in
Chapters 4 and 7.
Capitalisation
Historically again, the primary purpose of the doctorate has been to reproduce the
academic workforce, that is, to train new generations of researchers for the uni­
versities. However, this has changed, partly reﬂecting a paucity of tenured aca­
demic posts in some countries, the attractiveness of employment opportunities
outside academia, and a re-branding of the purpose of the doctorate to supply
human capital for the knowledge economies.
For these reasons, there is now a presumption that doctoral programmes will
include the development of the relevant generic skills to support doctoral gradu­
ates to gain employment inside or outside academia, So as well as their traditional
role in supporting candidates to becoming academics, supervisors may be expected
to support the development of the generic skills needed for them to enter a wider
range of employments.
The roles of supervisors in supporting candidates to prepare for academic and
non-academic roles are discussed in Chapter 14.

Conclusions
The effect of the changes outlined has been to transform doctoral education vir­
tually across the globe. What was the last ‘cottage industry’ in higher education
has become commodiﬁed, McDonaldised, collectivised, regulated, structured, and
increasingly collaborative; from being mono-disciplinary, many doctoral research
projects are interdisciplinary: as well as the traditional PhD, there are numerous
other types of doctorates; the precocious few candidates have become the diverse
many; obligations have come to the fore with regard to supervisors’ conduct
towards candidates and their responsibilities for their welfare; increasingly instead
of only studying on campus, candidates may be studying at a distance and being
examined online; doctoral studies have become increasingly directed towards
projects which are seen to have potential for a wider impact than academia; and,

The overall context

15

rather than becoming academics, doctoral graduates are increasingly engaged in
other forms of employment.
These changes have profoundly affected doctoral supervision, and there is some
evidence that this has been recognised by supervisors themselves. So, for example,
when Halse and Malfoy (2010: 80) conducted a survey of experienced doctoral
supervisors, they found that all of their respondents:
testiﬁed to the changes in their work. They described doctoral supervision
between the 1970s and the 1990s as an intellectual and social enterprise,
where personal boundaries were sometimes blurred but the roles of super­
visors and students were clear: the supervisor provided oversight and gui­
dance; the doctoral student was responsible for producing a seminal thesis that
would secure his or her place as an authority within the ﬁeld … all super­
visors … conceptualised their current work with doctoral students as sig­
niﬁcantly different from their former experiences. They spoke of actively
fashioning students’ development to address deﬁcits in expertise; deliberately
intervening to ensure timely completion; purposeful provision of tutoring or
research assistance to minimise the risk of ﬁnancial distress and withdrawal;
and the deployment of personal networks to facilitate completion and ensure
post-graduation employment.
The remainder of this book then seeks to assist supervisors in engaging with these
challenges and, where appropriate, to consider how to enhance their practice.

The institutional context

Introduction
The developments in doctoral education described in the previous chapter are
often only apparent to supervisors in their translation into the broad setting in
which they work, namely their institutions. It is the latter, of course, that ulti­
mately award doctoral degrees and are responsible for the standards of awards, for
setting expectations of supervisors, for deciding who is eligible to supervise, and
for providing the support necessary for them to supervise effectively.
Institutions can and do vary in all of these matters, and for this reason it is
important that supervisors do not simply rely upon their own experience to inform
their practice but make efforts to understand the institutional context within
which they are supervising.

The standards of doctoral awards
The ultimate object of the exercise is for the candidate to gain a doctorate, which
involves them meeting the standards set for the award. In order to supervise can­
didates to achieve those standards, supervisors obviously need to know what the
standards are. If they do not, then it effectively means that both are operating in
the dark, and this can be a recipe for disaster.
Of course, nearly all supervisors will have a doctorate themselves, and on this
basis they will have some understanding of the standards for the award. But,
bearing in mind that this is only a sample of one and may have been gained in a
different institution or a different doctoral programme or in a different higher
education system, they need to consult the formal deﬁnition.
In some countries with relatively centralised higher education systems, the
standards of doctorates are legally deﬁned by the state, as for example in France
(Batteau 2021) and Japan (Yamamoto 2021), but in others they are deﬁned by
institutions, and are usually set out in their regulations or ordinances.
Historically, these have not necessarily been the most enlightening sources of
information, but in recent years, institutions have been required by external

In response to these qualiﬁcations frameworks, institutions have been required
to specify explicit standards for their awards, including their doctoral ones. In
many cases, they have chosen to adopt national deﬁnitions. So, for example,
research by Taylor (2022) has shown that nearly all of the 131 UK institutions
with doctorate awarding powers have adopted the deﬁnition set out in the
national framework, either verbatim or in rephrased form.
So, rather than relying upon implicit interpretations of regulations, supervisors
should now be able to consult explicit institutional deﬁnitions to understand what
is required for the award. But it should be noted that this is not just helpful, but
necessary; while institutional deﬁnitions will have common core elements, they
may still vary in other respects.

Eligibility to supervise
In a few countries, eligibility to supervise is deﬁned by the state. So, for example,
in China academic staff must normally be full professors and have been pro­
moted to a higher grade in order to supervise (Lin and Liu, 2021). In France
(see Batteau 2021) and Poland (Kweik 2021) supervisors must by law have a
higher doctorate, the ‘habilitation’. In Sweden, it has been mandatory since
2004 for at least one supervisor to have attended the relevant institutional
training programme (Emilsson and Johnsson 2007).
In most countries, however, it is ultimately up to institutions to deﬁne who is
eligible to supervise doctoral students. Usually, it is a requirement that supervisors
themselves have a doctorate, although in some circumstances – particularly in
disciplines where the doctorate has been a relatively recent innovation – the
requirement may be for them to have equivalent research experience. A further
requirement in most cases that they are research active (see Taylor, Kiley, and
Holley 2021).
In many institutions, as well as the above, as will be seen in the next section,
there may be a further requirement that new supervisors undertake appropriate
initial professional development in the form of a course or training programme.
Additionally, some institutions may require supervisors to initially act as a
second/associate or junior supervisor in a team rather than as a primary supervisor.
Here, the normal requirement is to complete at least one supervisory cycle as a
second/associate supervisor under the wing of a more experienced senior super­
visor before becoming eligible to be a primary supervisor.
Finally, institutions may vary the number of candidates who can be supervised
on the basis of experience of successful completions; the more of the latter, the
more candidates can be supervised.

Example
Monash University in Australia uses a range of eligibility criteria to deﬁne
levels of accreditation for supervision. These are:
Registered status – this allows staff members or adjunct appointments
individuals to be an associate supervisor with a 10–25% supervisory load for
up to three students. To qualify, they need to have a doctorate or equivalent
research experience, and have completed required training.
Accredited status – this allows staff members or adjunct appointments to be
a main supervisor for up to eight candidates and an associate supervisor for a
further eight with a 10% to 90% supervisory load per student. To qualify, they
need to have a doctorate, at least one year’s full-time equivalent experience in
supervising a doctoral student or students, meet Faculty-speciﬁc quality and
research active criteria, and to have completed required training.
Accredited Emeritus status – this allows staff allows staff approaching
retirement to complete any current supervisions but not take on new

Support for supervisors
Support for supervisors may include initial professional development at the start of
their supervisory careers, opportunities for being mentored while acting as a
second supervisor, and opportunities for continuing professional development.
Additionally, institutions may also provide support in the event of difﬁculties in the
relationship with candidates, of which supervisors also need to be aware.
Initial professional development
Left to their own devices new supervisors tend to rely heavily upon their own
experiences as doctoral candidates to inform their practice (see for example
Amundsen and McAlpine 2009; Guerin, Kerr, and Green 2015, Fulgence, 2019,
Gonzalez-Ocampo and Castello 2019, Whittington, Barnes, and Lee 2021).
Where these experiences are good, they tend to emulate whatever their own
supervisors did; where they are poor, often they do the exact opposite. But neither
emulation nor rejection based on personal experience is likely to offer a sound
pedagogical basis upon which to be a successful supervisor, and there is evidence
(see for example Lovitts 2008, Wadesango and Machingambi 2011, Van de

28

The context

Schoot et al. 2013, Litalien and Guay 2015) that the quality of supervision is a
major factor in explaining delayed and non-completion of candidature.
Primarily for this reason many, if by no means all, institutions have estab­
lished mandatory initial professional development programmes for academic
staff who are new to supervision. This is almost universal in Australia (see Kiley
2021), Ireland (see Carton 2021), Sweden (see Brodin, Sonesson, and Ahlberg
2021), and the UK (Taylor 2018) but much patchier in other countries, for
example France (see Batteau 2021) or Germany (see Brentel 2021). In other
cases, there may be voluntary opportunities for professional development in
supervisory practice.
As Kiley (2021), Guerin et al. (2017) and Taylor (2018) have shown, there are
considerable variations in the duration and substance of initial professional devel­
opment programmes. The most basic generally lasts two or three hours and has a
focus on what might be described as compliance. In these sessions staff new to
research supervision are taken through the main rules and procedures relating to
being a supervisor at the particular institution, but not otherwise equipped for
their roles.

Example
Feather and McDermott (2014: 169) commented on a supervisor training
course they had been required to attend and reported that:
although [it] was insightful and engaging, we did not learn much more
than we already knew. The programme dealt mainly with the mechanics
(form-ﬁlling, administration, recording of attendance, and other
administrative tasks) of supervising PhDs, but not the realities of
supervision: that is, the emotions, the mentoring of students (both
domestic and international) and the softer issues of dealing with stu­
dents and their needs. So this left both of us with anxieties and ques­
tions concerning the supervision of doctoral students; more so as every
member of staff was expected to take on more doctoral students by the
school and the university.

Perhaps a more common format is the one-day introductory workshop/online
modules which, when completed, allow a staff member to take on a supervisory
role, although generally as a second/associate supervisor with a more experienced
colleague managing or chairing the overall supervisory team. Such programmes
may contain pedagogical elements, but often leave out other aspects of supervisory
responsibilities (see Taylor 2018).
In other cases, initial professional development programmes are much more
comprehensive.

However, while they may be comprehensive in meeting the needs of supervisors

doctorates. As will be seen in Chapter 4, supervising candidates on such programmes

provide dedicated support (see Hamilton and Carson 2015, Fillery-Travis et al. 2017).
It may be noted that, even where excellent professional development opportunities
are available, some new supervisors are highly resistant on the traditional grounds
that, by dint of achieving their own doctorate, they must by deﬁnition be capable of
supervising others to do the same (see Spiller, Byrnes, and Ferguson 2013) and/or
because they see doctoral supervision as a craft which can only be learned by actually
doing it (see Hockey 1997). But supervisors who are left to get on with it may end up
feeling out of their depth and unable to cope with the result it becomes a highly
stressful experience for them and for their candidates (see Hammond et al. 2010,
McAlpine 2013, Turner 2015). Moreover, there is some evidence that supervisor
development programmes can have a positive impact both upon supervisory practice
(see Bitzer 2010; Guerin, Kerr, and Green 2017; McCulloch and Loeser 2016,
Haven et al. 2022) and upon the student experience (see Brew and Peseta 2009).
This is not to say that initial professional development is a panacea for all of the issues
faced by new supervisors but it can help them to ﬁnd their feet in what is now a very
complex and demanding part of academic practice.

Conclusions
Hopefully, the present chapter has indicated the importance of supervisors being
aware of the institutional framework within which they are operating in terms of
standards, expectations, eligibility, and support. Often, these matters are covered
in induction or initial professional development workshops for new supervisors
who are thereby made aware of them at the time that they begin their supervisory
careers. But is worth noting that, as doctoral education itself is changing and is
impacted by events such as the Covid-19 pandemic, institutions are having to
follow suit, with the result that frameworks are constantly being modiﬁed and
updated. So deﬁnitions of the doctorate are being modiﬁed by the appearance of
new supra-national and national frameworks; expectations are constantly expand­
ing, for example through the new agendas of timely submission and completion,
the quality of the candidate experience, and employability; eligibility is becoming
more rigorous through the professionalisation of supervision; and support is
becoming more extensive, particularly in terms of professional development.
The point here is that it is not enough for supervisors to be aware of the insti­
tutional framework at whatever time they were taken through it in the past; they
need to keep up to date on current developments and, where appropriate, embody
them in their practice.
